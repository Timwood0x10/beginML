# **How Is the VC Dimension Derived? Why Is It So Important?**

## 🧮 How Is the VC Dimension Derived?

### Intuitive Understanding: Starting from "Labeling"

Imagine you have a pile of points and need to use a model to `label` them (+ or -). The **growth function** $\Pi_{\mathcal{H}}(n)$ is the maximum number of different labelings the model can produce for $n$ points.

**Example**: a straight-line classifier on the plane

- 1 point: can produce 2 labelings (+/-) $\to$ $\Pi=2=2^1$
  
  
- 2 points: can produce 4 labelings (++/+-/-+/--) $\to$ $\Pi=4=2^2$
  
  
- 3 points: can produce 8 labelings (all combinations) $\to$ $\Pi=8=2^3$
  
  
- 4 points: at most 14 labelings (the alternating + - + - labeling can't be separated) $\to$ $\Pi<2^4$

  

**VC dimension definition**: the maximum number of points the model can "shatter"
$$
d_{VC} = \max \{ n : \Pi_{\mathcal{H}}(n) = 2^n \}
$$


"Shattering" = being able to separate all $2^n$ labelings. So the VC dimension of a 2D linear classifier is 3.

### Rigorous Derivation: Four Steps

#### Step 1: Error Bound for a Single Model

- True error: 
  $$
  R(h) = P[h(X) \neq Y]
  $$
  (the final exam)
- Empirical error: 
  $$
  \hat{R}_N(h) = \frac{1}{N}\sum 1\{h(x_i) \neq y_i\}
  $$
  (the practice workbook)
- Hoeffding's inequality: 
  $$
  P(|R(h)-\hat{R}_N(h)|>\varepsilon) \leq 2e^{-2N\varepsilon^2}
  $$
  

#### Step 2: Handling a Family of Models (Union Bound)

The learning algorithm must pick the best model from the hypothesis space $\mathcal{H}$, so the bound must hold for all $h \in \mathcal{H}$ simultaneously.

Using the union bound:

$$
P(\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}|R(h)-\hat{R}_N(h)|>\varepsilon) \leq 2|\mathcal{H}|e^{-2N\varepsilon^2}
$$

But $\mathcal{H}$ is often infinite, so instead we look at **the number of distinct labelings it can produce on N points**:

$$
P(\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}|R(h)-\hat{R}_N(h)|>\varepsilon) \leq 2m_{\mathcal{H}}(N)e^{-2N\varepsilon^2}
$$

where $m_{\mathcal{H}}(N)$ is the growth function.

> tip: in a rigorous derivation, one uses the Symmetrization trick here, introducing another set of $N$ "ghost" samples. This changes the constants — e.g., the coefficient may become 8 and the exponent $N/8$ or similar, depending on the bound used. But the core conclusion — that the growth function controls the probability upper bound — remains unchanged.

#### Step 3: From the Growth Function to the VC Dimension (Sauer-Shelah Lemma)

Computing $m_{\mathcal{H}}(N)$ directly is hard, so we bound it using the VC dimension $d$. This requires an important result from combinatorics — the **Sauer-Shelah Lemma**.

##### Statement of the Sauer-Shelah Lemma

**Theorem (Sauer-Shelah Lemma)**:
Let the VC dimension of the hypothesis space $\mathcal{H}$ be $d$. Then for any $N \ge 1$:

$$
m_{\mathcal{H}}(N) \leq \sum_{i=0}^{d} \binom{N}{i}
$$

**Corollary (tighter bound)**:
When $N > d$:

$$
\sum_{i=0}^{d} \binom{N}{i} \leq \left(\frac{eN}{d}\right)^d
$$

##### Why Does This Lemma Hold? — Combinatorial Proof Sketch

**Core idea**: if the VC dimension is $d$, then any $d+1$ points cannot be shattered. This means that among $N$ points, every subset of $d+1$ points has a **missing** labeling pattern.

**Proof sketch**:

1. **Base case**: for $d=0$, $\mathcal{H}$ can only produce one labeling (all + or all -), so 
$$
m_{\mathcal{H}}(N) = 1 = \binom{N}{0}
$$
   

2. **Inductive step**: assume it holds for VC dimension $d-1$; prove it for VC dimension $d$.

3. **Key observation**: take any point $x$ among the $N$ points and split $\mathcal{H}$ into two parts:

   - $$
     \mathcal{H}_0 = \{h \in \mathcal{H} : h(x) = 0\}
     $$
   - $$
     \mathcal{H}_1 = \{h \in \mathcal{H} : h(x) = 1\}
     $$
4. **Recurrence relation**:

   $$
   m_{\mathcal{H}}(N) = m_{\mathcal{H}_0}(N-1) + m_{\mathcal{H}_1}(N-1)
   $$

   Note: the VC dimensions of both $\mathcal{H}_0$ and $\mathcal{H}_1$ are $\leq d-1$ (otherwise they could shatter $d+1$ points including $x$).
5. **Apply the inductive hypothesis**:
   $$
   m_{\mathcal{H}}(N) \leq \sum_{i=0}^{d-1} \binom{N-1}{i} + \sum_{i=0}^{d-1} \binom{N-1}{i} = \sum_{i=0}^{d} \binom{N}{i}
   $$

##### Intuitive Understanding

- **Small samples ($N \leq d$)**: the model is almost "omnipotent"; it can produce all $2^N$ labelings
  $$
  \sum_{i=0}^{N} \binom{N}{i} = 2^N
  $$
- **Large samples ($N > d$)**: the model's power is "capped" — growth drops from exponential to polynomial
  $$
  \sum_{i=0}^{d} \binom{N}{i} \approx \binom{N}{d} = O(N^d)
  $$

**Key insight**: the VC dimension $d$ acts like a "breaking point" — beyond it, the model's expressive power drops from exponential to polynomial!

##### Applied to the VC Generalization Bound

With the Sauer-Shelah Lemma, we have:

- If $N \leq d$: $m_{\mathcal{H}}(N) = 2^N$ (almost omnipotent)

- If $N > d$:

  $$
  m_{\mathcal{H}}(N) \leq \sum_{i=0}^{d}\binom{N}{i} \leq (\frac{eN}{d})^d
  $$

**Key**: the VC dimension "caps" exponential growth into polynomial growth!

#### Step 4: The VC Generalization Inequality

Substituting back gives:

$$
P(\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}|R(h)-\hat{R}_N(h)|>\varepsilon) \leq 4(\frac{2eN}{d})^d e^{-N\varepsilon^2/4}
$$

The equivalent high-probability form:

$$
R(h) \leq \hat{R}_N(h) + \sqrt{\frac{4}{N}[d\log\frac{2eN}{d} + \log\frac{4}{\delta}]}
$$

**Sample complexity**:

$$
N = O(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^2}[d\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon} + \log\frac{1}{\delta}])
$$

### Computing the VC Dimension of Common Models

#### Linear classifiers (d-dimensional space)

- **Model**: hyperplane classifier $f(x) = \text{sign}(w^T x + b)$
- **VC dimension**: $d + 1$
- **Detailed derivation**:

  **Step 1: prove VC dimension ≥ d+1**

  Take $d+1$ points in `general position`:

  $$
  x_0 = 0, \quad x_1 = e_1, \quad x_2 = e_2, \quad \ldots, \quad x_d = e_d
  $$

  For any labeling $(y_0, y_1, \ldots, y_d)$, we can find weights:

  $$
  w = \sum_{i=1}^d (y_i - y_0) e_i, \quad b = y_0
  $$

  Therefore, $d+1$ points can be shattered.

  **Step 2: prove VC dimension ≤ d+1**

  Suppose there are $d+2$ points. By linear algebra, there exist coefficients $\alpha_i$, not all zero, such that:

  $$
  \sum_{i=0}^{d+1} \alpha_i x_i = 0, \quad \sum_{i=0}^{d+1} \alpha_i = 0
  $$

  Considering two labelings leads to a contradiction, so $d+2$ points cannot be shattered arbitrarily.

  **Conclusion**: the VC dimension of a linear classifier is $d+1$.

#### Proving VC dimension $\le d+1$ (based on Radon's theorem)

1. Constructing linear dependence:

   Consider $d+2$ points $x_1, \ldots, x_{d+2}$ in $\mathbb{R}^d$. Since the number of points exceeds dimension+1, by linear algebra (affine dependence), there must exist real coefficients $\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_{d+2}$, not all zero, satisfying both conditions:

   $$
   \sum_{i=1}^{d+2} \alpha_i x_i = 0, \quad \text{and} \quad \sum_{i=1}^{d+2} \alpha_i = 0
   $$
2. Grouping and convex hulls (Radon decomposition):

   - By $\sum \alpha_i = 0$, the coefficients must contain both positive and negative values. Split the points into two groups:
   - Set $P$ (Positive): all points with $\alpha_i > 0$.
   - Set $N$ (Negative): all points with $\alpha_i < 0$.
     Rearranging the formula above:

   $$
   \sum_{i \in P} \alpha_i x_i = \sum_{j \in N} (-\alpha_j) x_j
   $$

   Let $S = \sum_{i \in P} \alpha_i = \sum_{j \in N} (-\alpha_j)$. Since $S > 0$, divide both sides by $S$:

   $$
   \sum_{i \in P} \frac{\alpha_i}{S} x_i = \sum_{j \in N} \frac{-\alpha_j}{S} x_j = \mathbf{z}
   $$

   Geometric meaning: the point $z$ is both a convex combination of points in $P$ and a convex combination of points in $N$.
   This means the convex hull of $P$ and the convex hull of $N$ must intersect (at point $z$).
3. Deriving the contradiction (separating hyperplane theorem)

   Now, suppose we label points in $P$ as +1 and points in $N$ as -1.
   If a linear hyperplane could fully separate these two classes, then by the **separating hyperplane theorem**, it must separate the convex hull of $P$ from the convex hull of $N$.
   But we proved in step 2 that the two convex hulls intersect at point $z$.
   Contradiction: a hyperplane cannot put $z$ on the positive side (because it's in $P$'s convex hull) and also on the negative side (because it's also in $N$'s convex hull).

Conclusion: a linear classifier cannot classify this particular labeling ($P$ positive, $N$ negative). Therefore, the model cannot shatter $d+2$ points, so VC dimension $\le d+1$.

#### Polynomial classifiers

- **Model**:

  $$
  f(x) = \text{sign}(p(x))
  $$

  , where

  $$
  p(x)
  $$

  is an

  $$
  n
  $$

  -degree polynomial
- **VC dimension**:

  $$
  \binom{n+d}{d}
  $$

  (a binomial coefficient)
- **Derivation**:

  The number of coefficients of an $n$-degree polynomial in $d$-dimensional space equals:

  $$
  \#\{I : |I| \le n\} = \binom{n+d}{d}
  $$

  **Key conclusion**: the VC dimension of a polynomial classifier equals the number of its free parameters.

#### Neural networks

- **Model**: feed-forward network with ReLU activation
- **VC dimension**: $O(W \log W)$, where $W$ is the number of weights
- **Derivation idea**:

  For ReLU networks, using the Sauer lemma and analysis of the growth function, one can prove the VC dimension is bounded above by:

  $$
  d_{VC} \le O(W \log W)
  $$

## Why Is the VC Dimension the Most Important Theory in Machine Learning?

### It Gives a Theoretical Guarantee for "Generalization Error"

For a model class with VC dimension $d_{VC}$ and a training set of size $N$, the generalization error $\epsilon$ has the following upper bound:

$$
\epsilon \le \epsilon_{\text{emp}} + \sqrt{\frac{d_{VC} (\log N + 1) - \log \eta}{N}}
$$

- $\epsilon_{\text{emp}}$: the empirical error (training error)
- $\eta$: the confidence level (e.g., 0.05, meaning it holds with 95% probability)

**Plain explanation**:

> As long as your model's VC dimension $d_{VC}$ is not infinite and you have enough training data $N$, you can guarantee that the model won't perform too badly on new data!
>

This is the "cornerstone" theory of machine learning!

### It Unifies the Notion of "Model Complexity"

Before the VC dimension, people measured model complexity in various ways:

- The number of parameters
- Goodness of fit
- AIC/BIC criteria

The VC dimension provides a **unified, universal** framework:

- **Complexity = VC dimension**
- **Generalization ability = determined jointly by VC dimension and data size**

### It Guides Modern Machine Learning Practice

- **SVM**: implicitly controls the VC dimension by maximizing the margin.
- **Regularization**: explicitly controls model complexity (lowers the effective VC dimension) via L1/L2 regularizers.
- **Deep learning**: although the theoretical VC dimension of deep networks is huge, in practice we effectively lower the "effective VC dimension" through early stopping, Dropout, BatchNorm, etc.

## Regularization — Forcing the VC Dimension's "Tight-Band" onto the Model

### SVM: A Born VC Dimension Expert

SVM's optimization objective is:

$$
\min \quad \underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\|w\|^2}_{\text{VC dimension control}} + C \sum_{i=1}^{n} \underbrace{\max(0, 1 - y_i f(x_i))}_{\text{hinge loss (empirical risk)}}
$$

#### Core Connection: Minimizing Weights = Minimizing VC Dimension

For linear classifiers (e.g., SVM), mathematical proofs show that **the VC dimension upper bound is inversely proportional to the margin**.

- **Maximum margin**$\rho$ = $\frac{2}{\|w\|}$
- **VC dimension (h) upper bound** $\propto \frac{1}{\rho^2} \propto \|w\|^2$

**Plain explanation**:

- **Smaller $\|w\|^2$** → **larger margin $\rho$** → **lower model complexity $h$**.

**SVM's "unity of heaven and man"**:

1. **Minimizing $\|w\|^2$**: by minimizing the squared weights, SVM **directly (albeit indirectly) minimizes the VC dimension $h$**, thereby controlling the **complexity penalty term** on the right side of the formula.
2. **Minimizing hinge loss**: minimizes the **empirical risk $R_{\text{emp}}(f)$**.

**Conclusion**: **SVM was the first algorithm to mathematically prove that "maximizing the margin" minimizes model complexity (VC dimension).**

### Regularization: A Universal VC Dimension Control Tool

The total loss with L2 regularization:

$$
\min \quad \underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \ell(y_i, f(x_i; w))}_{\text{empirical risk } R_{\text{emp}}} + \underbrace{\lambda \|w\|^2}_{\text{L2 regularizer}}
$$

In this formula, **the regularizer $\lambda \|w\|^2$ is a "proxy" for the VC dimension penalty term $\Phi(\frac{h}{n})$**.

- **The role of $\lambda$**:
  - **Larger $\lambda$**: heavier penalty on complexity; the model is forced to be simpler (VC dimension pushed down).
  - **Smaller $\lambda$**: we allow a more complex model that focuses more on fitting the training data.

### The Effective VC Dimension Formula

For a linear classifier with L2 regularization, the effective VC dimension is bounded above by:

$$
d_{VC}(\mathcal{J}_{L2}) = \min \left( \frac{R^2}{\rho^2},\ d \right) + 1 \quad \le d + 1
$$

- **$R^2$**: the maximum extent of the training data distribution.
- **$\rho^2$**: the square of the geometric margin.
- **$\frac{R^2}{\rho^2}$**: this ratio is a **measure of model complexity**. It decreases as the margin $\rho$ grows.

> This formula explains **`why SVM still works in high-dimensional spaces (large $d$)`** — even if the feature dimension $d \to \infty$ (e.g., an RBF kernel), as long as we can find a large margin $\rho$ (making $R^2 / \rho^2$ small), the model's actual complexity stays low. This is the mathematical reason SVM resists the `curse of dimensionality`.

**Core conclusion: regularization bounds the VC dimension through the margin $\rho$**

1. **L2 regularization** forces the model to find smaller $\|W\|^2$.
2. Smaller $\|W\|^2$ means **larger margin $\rho$**.
3. Larger margin $\rho$ means **smaller effective VC dimension $\frac{R^2}{\rho^2}$**.

## The VC Dimension and the Deep Learning Paradox

### The Contradiction Between Theory and Practice

#### Theoretical Prediction

- Deep neural networks have enormous parameter counts (millions to billions)
- The VC dimension should theoretically be extremely large (even infinite)
- According to VC theory, they should severely overfit

#### Actual Observation

- Deep networks perform well on training sets
- They also generalize well on test sets
- **The expected severe overfitting doesn't appear**

### Possible Explanations

#### Implicit Regularization

- **SGD's randomness**: stochastic gradient descent itself has a regularizing effect
- **Batching**: mini-batch training introduces noise, preventing overfitting
- **Early stopping**: in practice, training is usually stopped when validation performance degrades

#### The Advantage of Over-parameterization

- **Wider minimum basins**: more parameters make it easier to find flat minima
- **Faster optimization**: over-parameterization smooths the loss landscape
- **Benefits of interpolation**: fully interpolating the training data may help generalization

#### Modern Theoretical Developments

- **NTK (Neural Tangent Kernel) theory**: infinite-width networks are equivalent to kernel methods
- **The double descent phenomenon**: test error follows a double-U curve as model complexity grows

  - Classical regime: as complexity increases, test error first falls then rises (consistent with VC theory).
  - Interpolation Threshold: the point where training error reaches 0.
  - Modern regime: as complexity keeps growing (over-parameterization), test error falls again.
  - This is often explained as the model fitting a "minimum-norm solution" in this phase (similar to an L2-regularized solution as $\lambda \to 0$), so it's still subject to an implicit VC-dimension-like constraint.
- **The PAC-Bayes framework**: provides tighter generalization bounds

- Right side (modern regime): as parameters far exceed the sample count ($d \gg N$), the test error (red line) falls again. This proves that in the deep learning era, "over-parameterization" isn't bad — it can actually bring better generalization.

## Limitations of the VC Dimension

### Loose Bounds

#### Theoretical Bounds vs. Actual Performance

- **Theoretical upper bound**: $\epsilon \le \epsilon_{\text{emp}} + \sqrt{\frac{d_{VC} \log N}{N}}$
- **Practical issue**: this bound is usually very loose and overly conservative
- **Result**: the predicted generalization error is far larger than observed

#### A Numerical Example

- For ResNet-50 on ImageNet:
  - Parameters: ~26 million
  - Theoretical VC dimension: > $10^7$
  - Training samples: ~1 million
  - Theoretical prediction: should severely overfit
  - Actual performance: Top-1 error ~23%, generalizes well

### It Doesn't Consider the Data Distribution

#### Overly Pessimistic Assumptions

- The VC dimension assumes the **worst-case** data distribution
- Real data usually has structure; it isn't arbitrary
- **Result**: theoretical bounds are too conservative for real tasks

#### It Ignores Task Characteristics

- Different tasks vary greatly in difficulty
- The VC dimension doesn't consider label noise, feature correlation, etc.
- **Limitation**: can't give precise predictions for specific tasks

### It Doesn't Consider the Algorithm

#### Model Class vs. Concrete Algorithm

- The VC dimension only considers the model's **expressive power**
- It doesn't consider the **optimization algorithm's** characteristics
- **Problem**: different algorithms (SGD vs Adam) may have different generalization performance

## Practical Guidance from the VC Dimension

### Model Selection Principles

#### Choosing Complexity Based on Data Size

- **Rule of thumb**: $n \geq 10 \times d_{VC}$
- **Small datasets** ($n < 1000$):
  - Prefer simple models (linear, shallow trees)
  - Avoid deep networks; use strong regularization
- **Large datasets** ($n > 10^6$):
  - Complex models are fine
  - But still watch out for overfitting

#### The Importance of Cross-Validation

- **Theoretical guidance**: the VC dimension tells us complexity matters
- **Practical verification**: cross-validation actually assesses generalization
- **Combine both**: theory points the direction; validation confirms the result

### Regularization Strategies

#### Explicit Regularization

- **L1/L2 regularization**: directly controls model complexity
- **Dropout**: randomly masks neurons, lowering the effective VC dimension
- **Data augmentation**: increases effective data size, raising the allowable complexity

#### Implicit Regularization

- **Early stopping**: avoids overfitting the training data
- **Batch normalization**: stabilizes training, indirectly controlling complexity
- **Label smoothing**: prevents overconfidence, reducing overfitting risk

### Deep Learning Practice

#### Network Design Principles

- **Simple first, complex later**: start with a simple architecture, then gradually increase complexity
- **Width vs depth**: wider networks are usually easier to optimize than deeper ones
- **Residual connections**: help gradient flow, allowing deeper networks

#### Training Tricks

- **Learning rate scheduling**: a large initial learning rate has a regularizing effect
- **Weight decay**: the modern implementation of L2 regularization
- **Mixed precision**: may help generalization (still under research)

---

## Summary: A Modern View of the VC Dimension

### Theoretical Value

- **Foundational contribution**: the first systematic generalization theory
- **Unified framework**: connects model complexity with generalization ability
- **Guiding significance**: reminds us to pay attention to complexity control

### Practical Limitations

- **Too conservative**: the gap between theoretical bounds and practice is large
- **Doesn't consider optimization**: the optimization process matters a lot in modern deep learning
- **Needs development**: we need theories better suited to deep learning

### Future Directions

- **Tighter bounds**: Rademacher complexity, PAC-Bayes, etc.
- **Algorithm-aware**: generalization theories that account for the optimization process
- **Task-dependent**: generalization analysis combined with task characteristics

> **Core viewpoint**: although VC dimension theory has its limitations, its core idea — **model complexity must match data size** — remains the golden rule of machine learning.
