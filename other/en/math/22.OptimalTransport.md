# Optimal Transport

**Abstract**: In previous chapters, we discussed how to measure distances between vectors (Euclidean distance) and `differences` between probability distributions (KL divergence). However, when two distributions don't overlap, KL divergence fails (vanishing gradient). This chapter introduces **Optimal Transport (OT)** theory, which treats probability distributions as geometric objects and defines a metric on distribution space via the **Wasserstein distance**. We'll start from the classic earth-moving problem, derive the Kantorovich duality, analyze how the Sinkhorn algorithm turns the $O(N^3)$ hard problem into GPU-friendly matrix operations, and finally reveal its core role in WGAN and Flow Matching.

---

## 1. The Monge-Kantorovich Problem

### 1.1 Physical Intuition: The Earth-Moving Problem
Suppose you have a pile of dirt distributed over positions $x$, shaped as distribution $\mu(x)$; you want to move it to another location $y$, reshaping it into $\nu(y)$.
Moving a unit mass of dirt from $x$ to $y$ costs $c(x, y)$ (usually the Euclidean distance $||x-y||^2$).
**Core question**: how do you design a transport plan that minimizes the total cost?

### 1.2 The Monge Formulation (1781) - Deterministic Mapping
Monge looks for a mapping function $T: \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y}$ that ships all the dirt at $x$ to $T(x)$.
$$ \min_T \int_{\mathcal{X}} c(x, T(x)) d\mu(x) $$
The constraint is **mass conservation**: $\nu = T_{\#} \mu$ (i.e., $\nu$ is the pushforward of $\mu$ under $T$).
*   **Flaw**: if $\mu$ is a Dirac distribution (one point) and $\nu$ is two points, the Monge problem has no solution (you can't tear one point apart). This is a **non-convex** problem.

### 1.3 The Kantorovich Relaxation (1942) - Probabilistic Coupling
Kantorovich introduced the **Coupling Matrix** $\pi(x, y)$, representing how much mass is shipped from $x$ to $y$. This allows the dirt at $x$ to be distributed to different $y$'s.

$$ \min_{\pi} \int_{\mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y}} c(x, y) d\pi(x, y) $$

**Constraints**:
$$
\begin{cases}
\int_{\mathcal{Y}} \pi(x, y) dy = \mu(x) & \text{(row sum = } \mu \text{)} \\
\int_{\mathcal{X}} \pi(x, y) dx = \nu(y) & \text{(column sum = } \nu \text{)} \\
\pi(x, y) \ge 0
\end{cases}
$$
This becomes a **Linear Programming** problem, guaranteed to have a solution and to be convex.

## 2. The Wasserstein Distance: A Metric from the Geometric View

Based on the optimal transport cost, we define the $p$-Wasserstein distance:
$$ W_p(\mu, \nu) = \left( \inf_{\pi \in \Pi(\mu, \nu)} \mathbb{E}_{(x,y) \sim \pi} [||x - y||^p] \right)^{1/p} $$

### 2.1 Why Does KL Divergence Fail?
Consider two non-overlapping uniform distributions on the 2D plane (e.g., two separate rectangles).
*   **KL divergence**: $D_{KL}(P||Q) = \sum P(x) \log \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}$. If $Q(x)=0$ where $P(x)>0$, KL is $+\infty$. Even with tiny overlap, KL's gradient is local and can't tell $P$ which direction to move to get closer to $Q$.
*   **Wasserstein distance**: even if two distributions are far apart, $W_p$ still equals the physical distance between their centers.
    *   **Gradient property**: $\nabla_\theta W(P_\theta, Q)$ is constant and points in the right direction. This is the geometric essence of **WGAN** solving the vanishing-gradient problem.

### 2.2 The Kantorovich Duality
Solving directly for $\pi$ is computationally heavy. Using Lagrange multipliers, we can convert the primal problem into the dual problem:

$$ W_1(\mu, \nu) = \sup_{f, g} \left( \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mu}[f(x)] + \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \nu}[g(y)] \right) $$
Constraint: $f(x) + g(y) \le c(x, y)$.

When $c(x, y) = ||x-y||$, by the **Kantorovich-Rubinstein theorem**, the dual form simplifies to:
$$ W_1(\mu, \nu) = \sup_{||f||_L \le 1} \left( \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mu}[f(x)] - \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \nu}[f(x)] \right) $$
where $||f||_L \le 1$ means $f$ must be a **1-Lipschitz continuous** function.

*   **Physical meaning**: we shift from the `porter's view` (minimize shipping cost) to the `supplier's view` (maximize price difference). The function $f(x)$ acts like a potential field.

## 3. The Sinkhorn Algorithm: From Linear Programming to GPU Computation

Standard linear programming solving needs $O(N^3 \log N)$, which can't be applied to deep learning.
Cuturi (2013) proposed **Entropy Regularization**, completely changing the game.

### 3.1 The Entropy-Regularized Objective
$$ \min_{\pi \in \Pi} \langle C, \pi \rangle - \epsilon H(\pi) $$
where $H(\pi) = -\sum \pi_{ij} (\log \pi_{ij} - 1)$ is the Shannon entropy.

### 3.2 The Closed-Form Solution and Softmax
After introducing Lagrange multipliers, the optimal solution has the form:
$$ \pi_{ij} = u_i e^{-C_{ij}/\epsilon} v_j $$
This looks a lot like **Softmax**! Here $K_{ij} = e^{-C_{ij}/\epsilon}$ is the Gibbs Kernel.

### 3.3 The Sinkhorn-Knopp Iteration
We need to find vectors $u$ and $v$ so that $\pi$ satisfies the row and column constraints. This is an alternating projection process:
1.  **Row normalization**: $u^{(t+1)} = \mu / (K v^{(t)})$
2.  **Column normalization**: $v^{(t+1)} = \nu / (K^T u^{(t+1)})$

*   **Complexity**: this is just matrix-vector multiplication, $O(N^2)$, and fully parallelizable (GPU-friendly).
*   **Connection to Attention**: the **Attention mechanism** in Transformers is essentially a single-step Sinkhorn iteration (Query and Key compute the Cost, Softmax normalizes to get the transport matrix $\pi$). The Sinkhorn algorithm, through multiple iterations, seeks a Doubly Stochastic Matrix — a stricter form of `bidirectional attention`.

## 4. Applications: From WGAN to Flow Matching

Optimal transport theory doesn't just solve the metric problem; it also provides the optimal `evolution path` for generative models.

### 4.1 WGAN: Using the Duality
In GANs, we want to minimize $W_1(P_{data}, P_G)$. Since computing $W_1$ directly is hard, WGAN uses the **Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality** (see section 2.2).

$$ \min_G W_1(P_{data}, P_G) = \min_G \sup_{||f||_L \le 1} \left( \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P_{data}}[f(x)] - \mathbb{E}_{z \sim P_z}[f(G(z))] \right) $$

*   **The critic's role change**: the discriminator is no longer a binary classifier outputting probabilities $[0,1]$, but a regressor fitting the Lipschitz potential function $f(x)$. It tries to widen the score gap between real and fake samples.
*   **Lipschitz constraint**: to guarantee $||f||_L \le 1$, WGAN uses **Weight Clipping** or **Gradient Penalty (WGAN-GP)**: $(||\nabla_x f(x)||_2 - 1)^2$.

### 4.2 Flow Matching: Finding the Optimal Transport Map
Diffusion models define a random path from data to noise. Optimal transport tells us there's a **straight-line path (Geodesic)** connecting the two distributions with minimal transport cost.

**Flow Matching** (and its variant Rectified Flow) directly regress this straight-line velocity field:
Suppose we want to map noise $x_0 \sim N(0, I)$ to data $x_1 \sim P_{data}$.
The optimal transport displacement is $x_t = (1-t)x_0 + t x_1$.
The corresponding Vector Field is:
$$ v_t(x_t) = \frac{d}{dt} x_t = x_1 - x_0 $$

The training objective becomes regressing this velocity field:
$$ \mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{t, x_0, x_1} || v_\theta(x_t, t) - (x_1 - x_0) ||^2 $$

This converges faster than traditional Diffusion because it samples along the optimal transport path.

## 5. Code Practice: Implementing the Sinkhorn Algorithm

We'll manually implement the Sinkhorn iteration to solve the optimal transport plan between two 2D point-cloud distributions, and visualize the transport connections.

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import matplotlib.collections as mc

class OptimalTransportSolver:
    def __init__(self, epsilon=0.1, max_iter=100):
        self.epsilon = epsilon  # entropy regularization coefficient
        self.max_iter = max_iter

    def compute_cost_matrix(self, X, Y):
        """
        compute the Cost Matrix as squared Euclidean distance
        X: (N, d), Y: (M, d)
        C: (N, M)
        """
        # ||x - y||^2 = ||x||^2 + ||y||^2 - 2<x, y>
        x_col = np.sum(X**2, axis=1).reshape(-1, 1)
        y_row = np.sum(Y**2, axis=1).reshape(1, -1)
        C = x_col + y_row - 2 * np.dot(X, Y.T)
        return C

    def sinkhorn(self, C, mu, nu):
        """
        Sinkhorn-Knopp iteration to solve the coupling matrix P
        mu: source distribution weights (N,)
        nu: target distribution weights (M,)
        """
        N, M = C.shape
        K = np.exp(-C / self.epsilon) # Gibbs Kernel
        
        # initialize u, v
        u = np.ones(N)
        v = np.ones(M)
        
        for _ in range(self.max_iter):
            # row normalization: u = mu / (K @ v)
            u = mu / (np.dot(K, v) + 1e-8)
            # column normalization: v = nu / (K.T @ u)
            v = nu / (np.dot(K.T, u) + 1e-8)
            
        # P = diag(u) @ K @ diag(v)
        P = np.diag(u) @ K @ np.diag(v)
        return P

# ==========================================
# 1. generate the data
# ==========================================
np.random.seed(42)
n_points = 20

# source distribution: red points (around (-1, -1))
X = np.random.randn(n_points, 2) * 0.5 - 1
mu = np.ones(n_points) / n_points # uniform weights

# target distribution: blue points (around (1, 1))
Y = np.random.randn(n_points, 2) * 0.5 + 1
nu = np.ones(n_points) / n_points

# ==========================================
# 2. solve the optimal transport
# ==========================================
ot_solver = OptimalTransportSolver(epsilon=0.1, max_iter=50)
C = ot_solver.compute_cost_matrix(X, Y)
P = ot_solver.sinkhorn(C, mu, nu)

# compute the approximate Wasserstein distance
w_dist = np.sum(P * C)
print(f"Sinkhorn Distance (Approx W2): {w_dist:.4f}")

# ==========================================
# 3. visualize
# ==========================================
plt.figure(figsize=(8, 6))

# draw the points
plt.scatter(X[:, 0], X[:, 1], c='r', label='Source $\mu$', s=50, edgecolors='k')
plt.scatter(Y[:, 0], Y[:, 1], c='b', label='Target $\\nu$', s=50, edgecolors='k')

# draw the transport lines
# P_ij represents the amount transported from X_i to Y_j
# we only draw lines with transport amount above the threshold
lines = []
colors = []
threshold = 1e-4

for i in range(n_points):
    for j in range(n_points):
        if P[i, j] > threshold:
            lines.append([X[i], Y[j]])
            # color intensity represents the transport amount
            colors.append((0, 0, 0, P[i, j] * n_points)) 

lc = mc.LineCollection(lines, colors=colors, linewidths=1)
plt.gca().add_collection(lc)

plt.title(f"Optimal Transport Plan (Entropy Regularized $\epsilon$={ot_solver.epsilon})")
plt.legend()
plt.grid(True, linestyle='--', alpha=0.5)
plt.axis('equal')
plt.show()

# show the coupling matrix heatmap
plt.figure(figsize=(5, 5))
plt.imshow(P, cmap='viridis')
plt.title("Coupling Matrix $\pi$")
plt.colorbar()
plt.show()
```

#### Analysis and Summary of Experimental Results

![op_transport](../images/op_transport.png)


**Reading the figure:**
The figure above shows the optimal plan for transporting source distribution $\mu$ (red points) to target distribution $\nu$ (blue points) under the entropy regularization coefficient $\epsilon=0.1$. The intensity of the gray connecting lines represents the magnitude of the coupling matrix $\pi_{ij}$ (i.e., the share of transported mass).

**Core insights:**

1.  **Locality**:
    Looking at the connections, red points tend to connect to their nearest blue points. This verifies the **geometric property** of optimal transport — it automatically finds the global optimum minimizing total transport distance ($\sum \pi_{ij} ||x_i - y_j||^2$), rather than random pairing.

2.  **The `blurring effect` of entropy regularization**:
    Notice that some red points (e.g., those in the middle region) don't connect to just one blue point; they emit several lines of varying intensity to nearby blue points.
    *   **Mathematical reason**: this is the effect of the $\epsilon H(\pi)$ term. Regularization makes the transport plan no longer an absolute `hard assignment` (0 or 1), but a `soft assignment`.
    *   **Trade-off**: $\epsilon=0.1$ maintains high transport accuracy while giving the optimization problem convexity, letting Sinkhorn converge quickly. If $\epsilon \to 0$, the connections become sparse one-to-one mappings; if $\epsilon \to \infty$, the connections become uniformly fully connected.

3.  **Implications for generative models**:
    Imagine these gray connections are the trajectories of countless particles moving over time.
    *   **WGAN**: the total length of these connections (weighted sum) is the Wasserstein distance — the `scalar` the WGAN critic tries to estimate.
    *   **Flow Matching / Diffusion**: if we want to train a neural network to turn red points into blue points, **the optimal strategy is to make the network fit the vector field of these gray connections**. This is the geometric reason modern generative models (like Rectified Flow) are more efficient than traditional Diffusion — they sample along the `geodesic` of optimal transport.

**Reading the figure:**
The figure above shows the $20 \times 20$ coupling matrix $\pi$.
*   **Horizontal axis**: indices of target points $\nu$ (0-19).

    Most of the figure is dark purple (near 0). This means that although Sinkhorn is fully connected in computation (like Softmax), after convergence, **most transport weights automatically go to zero**.
    *   **Mathematical meaning**: the optimal transport plan tends to be "monogamous." A source point usually only needs to supply 1-2 target points, not spread itself evenly. This sparsity is very important for computational efficiency on large-scale data.

2.  **Satisfying the doubly stochastic constraint**:
    Although hard to sum precisely by eye, the algorithm guarantees that **each row sums to $1/N$** and **each column also sums to $1/N$**. Although the bright blocks look scattered, they achieve perfect mathematical balance along both rows and columns.

3.  **The physical prototype of an Attention Map**:
    If you're familiar with Transformers, this figure looks very familiar. **It's essentially an Attention Map**.
    *   In Transformers, the attention score of Token A toward Token B equals the "mass" transported from point A to point B here.
    *   The **entropy regularization coefficient $\epsilon$** plays the role of **Temperature ($\tau$)**. The smaller $\epsilon$, the more concentrated the bright blocks on the heatmap (One-hot), corresponding to "sharper" Attention; the larger $\epsilon$, the more uniform the heatmap, corresponding to "smoother" Attention.

## 6. Summary: The Bridge Between Geometry and Probability

Optimal transport theory not only gives us a powerful tool for measuring distribution distance (Wasserstein Distance); more importantly, it geometricizes probability problems.

From KL to Wasserstein: we no longer merely compare the numerical differences of two probability density functions at the same point; we compute the physical work needed to `deform` one distribution into another.
From Diffusion to Flow: generative models are no longer blind random walks; they evolve deterministically along the geodesic of optimal transport.

At this point, our mathematical puzzle has filled in the **metric** piece. In the next chapter, we'll enter the world of **games**, exploring how to find equilibrium when the optimization target is no longer fixed but changes with the opponent's strategy.
