# The Intrinsic Connection Between Hilbert Spaces, the Fourier Transform, and Neural Networks

## **⚠️ Important Technical Clarification: Continuous Theory vs. Discrete Implementation**

### **1. Mathematical Rigor of the Fourier Transform**

- **Continuous space**: $\mathcal{F}: L^2(\mathbb{R}^d) \to L^2(\mathbb{R}^d)$ is a unitary operator strictly defined on an **infinite-dimensional Hilbert space**
- **Parseval's identity**: $\langle f, g \rangle = \langle \hat{f}, \hat{g} \rangle$ guarantees **energy conservation** — the core mathematical foundation of signal processing
- **Theoretical significance**: the continuous Fourier transform provides solid mathematical support for CNN rigor

### **2. The Engineering Practice of Discretization**

- **DFT/FFT**: real applications use the discrete Fourier transform, which is also a unitary transform on the finite-dimensional space $\mathbb{C}^N$
- **Sampling theorem**: ensures the continuous-to-discrete conversion loses no key information
- **Numerical computation**: the FFT algorithm reduces convolution complexity from $O(n^2)$ to $O(n \log n)$

### **3. Frequency-Domain Implementation of Convolution**

- **Strict equivalence**: under appropriate boundary conditions, time-domain convolution equals frequency-domain multiplication
- **Diagonalization condition**: a convolution kernel's frequency-domain representation is a diagonal matrix — a property that **directly derives from translation invariance**
- **Applicability**: this property **only applies to convolution operations**, not to other neural network layers like fully connected layers

These technical details ensure a balance between mathematical rigor and engineering practicality.

## **1. The Mathematical Definition of a Hilbert Space (and Its NN Correspondence)**

### **1. Basic Definition**

A **Hilbert space** $\mathcal{H}$ is a complete inner-product space:

- **Inner product**: $\langle f, g \rangle$ measures the similarity between two functions
- **Completeness**: every Cauchy sequence in the space converges to a point in the space (guaranteeing stable optimization)

**NN correspondence**: input data $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$ can be seen as a vector in a finite-dimensional Hilbert space; for continuous signals (like images, audio), they belong to the infinite-dimensional Hilbert space $L^2(\mathbb{R}^d)$.

---

## **2. The Fourier Transform as a Unitary Operator on a Hilbert Space**

### **2.1 The Function-Space Setting**

Consider the space of square-integrable functions:

$$
L^2(\mathbb{R}^d) = \left\{ f: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{C} \,\bigg|\, \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} |f(x)|^2 \,dx < \infty \right\}
$$

This is an **infinite-dimensional Hilbert space**, with the inner product defined as:

$$
\langle f, g \rangle = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} f(x) \overline{g(x)} \,dx
$$

**NN correspondence**: a normalized image $I(x,y)$ satisfies $\int I^2(x,y)dxdy < \infty$, so $I \in L^2(\mathbb{R}^2)$.

---

### **2.2 The Strict Definition of the Continuous Fourier Transform**

The continuous Fourier transform $\mathcal{F}: L^2(\mathbb{R}^d) \to L^2(\mathbb{R}^d)$ is defined as:

$$
\mathcal{F}[f](\omega) = \hat{f}(\omega) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} f(x) e^{-i\langle \omega, x \rangle} \,dx
$$

**Key property**: $\mathcal{F}$ is a **Unitary Operator**, i.e., it preserves the inner product:

$$
\langle f, g \rangle = \langle \hat{f}, \hat{g} \rangle \quad \text{(Parseval's identity)}
$$

This means the continuous Fourier transform **doesn't change the energy of the function** — it only **rotates the coordinate system** in the Hilbert space.

- Connection: Parseval's theorem guarantees energy conservation (norm preservation).
- This connects to LayerNorm / BatchNorm in neural networks.
  - BN/LN's job is exactly to force each layer's output back onto (or near) a unit sphere in the Hilbert space.
  - Without normalization, as layers deepen, the cumulative effect of operators could cause the signal's norm in Hilbert space to explode (gradient explosion) or shrink to zero (gradient vanishing).

### **2.3 The Practicality of the Discrete Fourier Transform**

In real applications, we usually use the **Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT)**:

$$
\hat{f}[k] = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} f[n] e^{-i2\pi kn/N}
$$

**Important differences**:

- **Continuous space**: $L^2(\mathbb{R}^d)$ is an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space
- **Discrete space**: $\mathbb{C}^N$ is a finite-dimensional Hilbert space
- **DFT property**: the discrete Fourier transform is also a unitary transform, but on a different domain

**Why $L^2$ space matters**:

- **Parseval's theorem**: guarantees energy conservation (the core of signal processing)
- **Completeness**: ensures the Fourier transform is invertible
- **Mathematical rigor**: provides solid support for CNN's mathematical foundation

## **3. A Rigorous Derivation of the Convolution Theorem (the Core of CNN)**

### **3.1 Definition of Convolution**

In $L^2(\mathbb{R}^d)$, the convolution of two functions is defined as:

$$
(f * g)(x) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} f(y)g(x-y) \,dy
$$

**NN correspondence**: $f$ is the input image, $g$ is the convolution kernel (e.g., a 3×3 filter).

---

### **3.2 Proof of the Convolution Theorem**

**Theorem**: $\mathcal{F}[f * g] = \mathcal{F}[f] \cdot \mathcal{F}[g]$

**Derivation**:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{F}[f * g](\omega) &= \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} (f * g)(x) e^{-i\langle \omega, x \rangle} \,dx \\
&= \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \left( \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} f(y)g(x-y) \,dy \right) e^{-i\langle \omega, x \rangle} \,dx \\
&\stackrel{\text{Fubini's theorem}}{=} \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} f(y) \left( \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} g(x-y) e^{-i\langle \omega, x \rangle} \,dx \right) dy \\
&\stackrel{z = x-y}{=} \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} f(y) e^{-i\langle \omega, y \rangle} \left( \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} g(z) e^{-i\langle \omega, z \rangle} \,dz \right) dy \\
&= \left( \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} f(y) e^{-i\langle \omega, y \rangle} \,dy \right) \cdot \left( \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} g(z) e^{-i\langle \omega, z \rangle} \,dz \right) \\
&= \hat{f}(\omega) \cdot \hat{g}(\omega)
\end{aligned}
$$

**Conclusion**: time-domain convolution = frequency-domain pointwise multiplication

---

### **3.3 Frequency-Domain Implementation of Convolution**

For one CNN layer:

$$
h_{l+1} = \sigma(W_l * h_l + b_l) \quad \text{(time-domain convolution)}
$$

**Mathematical equivalence** (theoretical level):

$$
\hat{h}_{l+1} = \sigma(\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\hat{W}_l \odot \hat{h}_l) + b_l) \quad \text{(frequency-domain multiplication)}
$$

**⚠️ Important reminder**:

- This is only a mathematical equivalence
- Real CNN implementations don't use frequency-domain multiplication
- The frequency-domain view is mainly for theoretical analysis and understanding

**Key insights**:

- **Frequency-domain representation**: the Fourier transform of the kernel $\hat{W}_l$ is a diagonal operator in the frequency domain
- **Translation invariance**: convolution's translation invariance shows up in the frequency domain as a phase change, while the magnitude stays constant
- **Diagonalization advantage**: frequency-domain pointwise multiplication is computationally more efficient than time-domain sliding-window convolution

**Eigenfunctions**:

- In Hilbert space operator theory, if an operator $T$ acting on a function $f$ merely multiplies it by a constant $\lambda$ (i.e., $Tf=\lambda f$), then $f$ is an eigenfunction of $T$.
- The complex exponential $e^{i\omega x}$ is exactly the eigenfunction of translation-invariant linear operators (convolutions).
- This is why the Fourier transform turns convolution into multiplication — because the Fourier transform essentially decomposes the signal onto the eigenbasis of the convolution operator. On the eigenbasis, a linear operator naturally appears as a diagonal matrix. This fundamentally explains `why the Fourier transform, and not some other transform`.

### **3.4 Discretization Considerations**

In real CNN implementations:

- **DFT/FFT**: uses the discrete Fourier transform to approximate the continuous one
- **Sampling theorem**: ensures the continuous-to-discrete conversion loses no key information
- **Boundary handling**: padding handles finite-length boundary effects
- **Numerical stability**: frequency-domain computation is usually more stable than time-domain convolution

### **3.5 Actual Engineering Implementation of Frequency-Domain Convolution**

**⚠️ Important clarification: CNNs are NOT implemented in the frequency domain**

**Implementation comparison**:

- **Direct time-domain convolution**: most CNNs use this (PyTorch, TensorFlow default)
- **Frequency-domain FFT convolution**: used only for large kernels or specific optimization scenarios (e.g., FlashConv)

**Mathematical equivalence vs. engineering implementation**:

- **Cyclic convolution vs. linear convolution**: FFT implements cyclic convolution; CNNs use linear convolution + padding
- **Equivalence condition**: proper padding is needed to make linear convolution equivalent to cyclic convolution
- **Engineering reality**: CNNs are usually implemented directly in the time domain; frequency-domain understanding is mainly for theoretical analysis

**Frequency-domain understanding in real CNNs**:

- **Filter spectrum**: trained kernels typically show **low-pass characteristics**
- **Frequency selectivity**: deep networks tend to learn finer frequency structures
- **Optimization goal**: the network implicitly learns the frequency representation suited to the task
- **Engineering value**: the frequency view helps understand why CNNs work, but it isn't the actual implementation

**Engineering practice**:

```python
# actual CNN implementation (time domain)
import torch.nn as nn

class CNN(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self):
        self.conv = nn.Conv2d(3, 64, padding=1)  # padding ensures linear convolution
        self.relu = nn.ReLU()
  
    def forward(self, x):
        return self.relu(self.conv(x))  # time-domain convolution, not frequency-domain multiplication
```

### 3.6 The Industry's Third Path: The Winograd Algorithm

In high-performance deep learning libraries like NVIDIA cuDNN, the most common small $3×3$ kernels usually use neither the intuitive "sliding window" (direct convolution) nor a huge FFT, but the Winograd minimal filtering algorithm.

#### Mathematical essence

The Winograd algorithm is also a transform-domain convolution. Through a specific linear transformation matrix $G$ (similar to the Fourier transform matrix, but defined over the reals), it maps the input signal onto a new basis.

$$
Y = A^T [(G g G^T) \odot (B^T d B)] A
$$

where $g$ is the convolution kernel, $d$ is the input data, $A, G, B$ are fixed transformation matrices, and $\odot$ is pointwise multiplication.

#### Core advantages

- **Trading multiplications for additions**: in computer hardware, multiplication instructions cost far more than addition. Winograd uses clever transforms to drastically reduce the number of multiplications (at the cost of more additions and memory usage).
- **Real-domain operations**: unlike FFT, which must enter the complex domain (requiring storing real and imaginary parts, doubling memory), Winograd operates entirely over the reals.

#### Connection to Hilbert spaces

This again confirms the core idea of functional analysis — **choosing the right basis**.

- **FFT**: chooses the complex exponential basis; good for large kernels (global features).
- **Winograd**: chooses a specific polynomial basis; good for small kernels (local features).

Both are transformation operators that `diagonalize` (simplify) the convolution operation in a Hilbert space.

## **4. Neural Network Optimization from a Hilbert Space Perspective**

### **4.1 The Function-Approximation View**

The target function $f^*$ that a neural network learns belongs to a Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}$. The network approximates it by minimizing a loss:

$$
\min_{f \in \mathcal{H}} || f - f^* ||^2 = \min_{f \in \mathcal{H}} \langle f - f^*, f - f^* \rangle
$$

### **4.2 Gradient Descent in Hilbert Space Form**

In a Hilbert space, gradient descent updates as:

$$
f_{t+1} = f_t - \eta \nabla_{\mathcal{H}} \mathcal{L}(f_t)
$$

where $\nabla_{\mathcal{H}}$ is the **Hilbert gradient**. By the Riesz representation theorem, the linear functional $D\mathcal{L}(f)$ corresponds uniquely to an element in the Hilbert space — the element we call the `gradient`. It satisfies:

$$
\langle \nabla_{\mathcal{H}} \mathcal{L}(f), v \rangle = D\mathcal{L}(f)[v], \quad \forall v \in \mathcal{H}
$$

**NN correspondence**: backpropagation is exactly computing this Hilbert gradient.

---

### **4.3 A Hilbert Space Explanation of Regularization**

L2 regularization:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{reg}} = \mathcal{L} + \lambda ||W||^2
$$

where $\|W\|^2 = \langle W, W \rangle$ is the **squared norm** of the weights in Hilbert space.

This is equivalent to finding the **minimum-norm solution** in Hilbert space:

$$
\min_{W} \mathcal{L}(W) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad ||W|| \leq C
$$

## **5. A Unified Framework for Spectral Methods and Neural Networks**

### **5.1 The Mathematical Basis of the Graph Fourier Transform**

For graph neural networks, define the eigendecomposition of the graph Laplacian matrix $L$:
$
L = U \Lambda U^T
$
where $U$ is an orthogonal basis (unitary matrix) and $\Lambda$ is the diagonal eigenvalue matrix.

#### **Definition of the Graph Fourier Transform**

The graph Fourier transform:

$$
\hat{x} = U^T x
$$

Graph convolution (spectral convolution):

$$
g_\theta * x = U g_\theta(\Lambda) U^T x
$$

#### **A Physical Interpretation of "Frequency"**

- **Graph frequency**: the eigenvalue $\lambda_i$ corresponds to the characteristic frequency of the Laplacian operator. In images, `high frequency` means edges and textures; in graphs, "high frequency" means outlier nodes or cross-community connections (a node differing greatly from its neighbors).
- **Meaning of "frequency"**: it represents the oscillation mode of the graph structure at that characteristic frequency
- **Intuition**: high eigenvalues correspond to "rapidly changing" graph patterns (adjacent nodes differ a lot); low eigenvalues correspond to "slowly changing" patterns (adjacent nodes are similar)
- **Engineering explanation**: frequency = the rate of change of a signal on the graph, not a spatial geometric frequency

#### **Difference from CNN frequency domain**:

- **CNN frequency**: based on spatial geometric frequency (e.g., $\sin(\omega x)$)
- **Graph frequency**: based on graph structural features (e.g., eigenvalue $\lambda_i$)
- **Mathematical essence**: both are orthogonal bases in a Hilbert space, but the basis functions are completely different

#### **Engineering practicality**:

- **Eigendecomposition**: $L = U \Lambda U^T$ provides the optimal graph frequency representation
- **Convolution simplification**: spectral convolution turns complex graph convolution into simple pointwise multiplication
- **Interpretability**: eigenvalue size reflects that mode's "importance"**

### **5.2 Transformer Positional Encoding**

The Transformer uses sinusoidal/cosine positional encoding:

$$
PE_{(pos,2i)} = \sin(pos / 10000^{2i/d}), \quad PE_{(pos,2i+1)} = \cos(pos / 10000^{2i/d})
$$

**⚠️ A precise statement about orthogonality**:

- **Theoretical orthogonality**: in the continuous space $L^2([0,1])$, $\langle \sin(kx), \cos(mx) \rangle = 0$ when $k,m$ are integers
- **Actual implementation**:

  - In the continuous limit, $\sin(\omega_1 x)$ and $\sin(\omega_2 x)$ are indeed orthogonal (if $\omega$ takes integers).
  - The Transformer's frequencies are distributed exponentially as $1/10000^{2i/d}$. This design isn't just for orthogonality — this frequency distribution forms a geometric sequence.
  - This lets the network easily infer the features of position $pos+k$ from position $pos$ through a simple linear transformation (learning the Attention matrix), using rotation-matrix properties (like the RoPE derivation). The core here is less about Hilbert space orthogonality and more about **rotation properties in a Lie Group**.
- **Engineering significance**: it forms a multi-scale frequency basis, making different positional encodings **approximately linearly independent** in Hilbert space

**Mathematical rigor**:

- It's not a strict Fourier orthogonal basis
- But it provides **spectral coverage** from low to high frequency
- The attention mechanism can effectively exploit this multi-scale frequency pattern for position discrimination

## **6. Engineering Practicality: A Bridge Between Theory and Reality**

### **6.1 The Gap Between Continuous Theory and Discrete Implementation**

**Theoretical assumptions vs. engineering reality**:

- **Theoretical image**: belongs to the continuous space $L^2(\mathbb{R}^2)$
- **Actual image**: belongs to the discrete space $\mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times C}$
- **Sampling effects**: introduce aliasing; high-frequency information may be lost
- **Boundary handling**: padding is an engineering choice, not a mathematical necessity

### **6.2 The Practical Value of the CNN Frequency View**

**Why are trained filters mostly low-pass?**

- **Natural image characteristics**: most energy concentrates at low frequencies
- **Optimization preference**: low-pass filters are more stable and generalize better
- **Hierarchical learning**: shallow layers learn basic frequencies, deep layers learn fine frequencies

**Visualization example**:

### **6.3 Trade-offs in Real Applications**

**Computational efficiency vs. mathematical rigor**:

- **Small-kernel convolution**: direct time-domain implementation is more efficient
- **Large-kernel convolution**: frequency-domain FFT may be better (e.g., 31×31 and above)
- **Memory considerations**: the frequency domain needs extra storage for complex numbers
- **Hardware optimization**: GPUs are highly optimized for time-domain convolution

### **6.4 Frequency-Domain Characteristics of Different Architectures**

**CNN**:

- Translation invariance $\to$ frequency-domain diagonalization
- Local connectivity $\to$ frequency-domain localization
- Hierarchical structure $\to$ multi-scale frequency analysis

**GNN**:

- Graph-structure dependence $\to$ Laplacian eigenbasis
- Non-Euclidean $\to$ generalized frequency concept
- Spectral methods $\to$ high computational complexity

**Transformer**:

- Global attention $\to$ no locality assumption
- Positional encoding $\to$ approximately orthogonal frequency basis
- Adaptivity $\to$ learns frequency-domain weights

## **7. Ultimate Summary: From Formulas to Intuition**

### **7.1 The Mathematical Chain**

$$
\text{neural network} \xrightarrow{\text{each layer's operation}} \text{Hilbert space operator} \\
\text{convolution layer} \xrightarrow{\mathcal{F}} \text{frequency-domain diagonal multiplication} \\
\text{optimization} \xrightarrow{\text{Riesz representation theorem}} \text{Hilbert space gradient descent}
$$

### **7.2 Core Formula Summary**

1. **Hilbert space inner product**: $\langle f, g \rangle = \int f(x)\overline{g(x)}dx$
2. **Fourier transform**: $\hat{f}(\omega) = \int f(x)e^{-i\omega x}dx$
3. **Convolution theorem**: $\widehat{f * g} = \hat{f} \cdot \hat{g}$
4. **One CNN layer**: $h_{l+1} = \sigma(\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\hat{W}_l \odot \hat{h}_l) + b_l)$

### **7.3 Intuitive Understanding**

- **Hilbert space**: the neural network's **mathematical universe**, where all operations happen
- **Fourier transform**: **rotates the coordinate system** in this universe, making convolution simple
- **Convolution theorem**: after rotation, complex convolution becomes **multiplication a schoolchild could do**
- **Optimization**: walk downhill in this universe along the **steepest direction** (the gradient)

### **7.4 Core Insights for Engineering Practice**

- **Theory guides practice**: mathematical theory helps us understand why neural networks work
- **Practice corrects theory**: engineering implementations must consider computational efficiency and hardware limits on top of theory
- **Value of the frequency view**: not for direct implementation, but for understanding and designing better architectures
- **The art of balance**: find the balance between mathematical rigor and engineering practicality
