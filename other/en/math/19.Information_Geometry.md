# Information Geometry and Natural Gradients: Optimization on Riemannian Manifolds

**Why is the gradient in parameter space usually wrong?**

In previous chapters, we usually treated the parameters $\theta$ as a point in Euclidean space. But in deep learning, **the numerical distance between parameters doesn't represent the behavioral distance between models**.

This chapter reveals the true playing field of optimization — the **Riemannian Manifold** — and proves that adaptive optimizers like Adam essentially perform an `inverse transformation` on the space's curvature.

---

## 1. Intuition: Maps and the Globe

### 1.1 Parameter Space $\neq$ Probability Space
Suppose we have two Gaussians $p = \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$.

*   **Case A**: $\sigma = 10$. $\mu$ changes from $0$ to $1$. The distributions barely change (extremely high overlap).
*   **Case B**: $\sigma = 0.01$. $\mu$ changes from $0$ to $1$. The distributions become completely separated.

**The contradiction**: in parameter space, both displacements are $\|\Delta \mu\| = 1$. But on the probability manifold, Case B's traveled distance is far greater than Case A's.

**Conclusion**: using the Euclidean distance $\|\theta - \theta'\|^2$ to measure a model's update step is **broken**. We need a new metric.

### 1.2 KL Divergence as the `Ruler`
We care about **changes in the distribution**, not **changes in parameter values**. A rigorous distance metric should be based on KL divergence:

$$
D_{KL}(p_\theta \| p_{\theta+\delta}) \approx \frac{1}{2} \delta^T \mathbf{F}(\theta) \delta
$$

This quadratic form reveals the local geometric structure of the space, where $\mathbf{F}$ is the metric tensor.

---

## 2. The Fisher Information Matrix (FIM)

$\mathbf{F}$ is the meeting point of probability statistics and differential geometry.

### 2.1 Mathematical Definition
$\mathbf{F}$ is defined as the **covariance matrix of the log-likelihood's gradient**:

$$
\mathbf{F} = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p(x|\theta)} \left[ \nabla_\theta \log p(x|\theta) \nabla_\theta \log p(x|\theta)^T \right]
$$

### 2.2 Geometric Meaning: Local Curvature
*   **Large $\mathbf{F}$**: gradients change violently, the distribution is sensitive to parameters $\to$ the space is highly curved $\to$ should take small steps.
*   **Small $\mathbf{F}$**: gradients are gentle, the distribution is insensitive to parameters $\to$ the space is flat $\to$ can take big steps.

Geometrically, $\mathbf{F}$ defines an **elliptical** neighborhood. Ordinary gradient descent treats the surroundings as a perfect circle, while $\mathbf{F}$ tells us it's a stretched ellipse.

---

## 3. Natural Gradient Descent

If we want to take fixed-length steps on the **probability manifold** rather than wandering blindly in parameter space, we need to solve the following constrained optimization problem:

$$
\min_{\delta} L(\theta + \delta) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad D_{KL}(p_\theta \| p_{\theta+\delta}) = c
$$

### 3.1 The Derived Result
Using Lagrange multipliers, we get the **natural gradient** update formula:

$$
\tilde{\nabla} L = \mathbf{F}^{-1} \nabla L
$$

$$
\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta \mathbf{F}^{-1} \nabla L(\theta_t)
$$

### 3.2 How It Works: Preconditioning
$\mathbf{F}^{-1}$ cancels out the space's curvature:
*   If a direction has extremely high curvature (large $\mathbf{F}$), $\mathbf{F}^{-1}$ shrinks the gradient in that direction.
*   If a direction is very flat (small $\mathbf{F}$), $\mathbf{F}^{-1}$ amplifies the gradient in that direction.

This turns the optimization path from `zigzag` oscillation into a `geodesic` pointing straight at the center.

---

## 4. Why Does Adam Work? — The Diagonal Approximation of the FIM

In deep learning, computing and inverting $\mathbf{F}$ (an $N \times N$ matrix) is infeasible.

### 4.1 The Essence of RMSProp / Adam
Adam maintains an exponential moving average of squared gradients $v_t$:

$$
v_t \approx \mathbb{E}[(\nabla L)^2]
$$

If we assume the parameters are mutually independent (i.e., ignoring correlations), then **the FIM degenerates into a diagonal matrix**, whose diagonal elements are exactly the second moments of the gradients.

Adam's update rule (simplified):

$$
\Delta \theta \propto -\frac{1}{\sqrt{v_t}} \nabla L \approx -\frac{1}{\sqrt{\text{diag}(\mathbf{F})}} \nabla L
$$

### 4.2 Conclusion
**Adam is essentially a `diagonal approximation` of natural gradient descent.**
Through the $1/\sqrt{v_t}$ term, it adaptively scales the step size in each dimension, trying to restore the distorted elliptical space to a perfect circle.

## 5. Code and Visualization: SGD vs. Natural Gradient

This experiment fits a 2D Gaussian, intuitively showing the inefficiency of Euclidean gradients (SGD) versus the directness of natural gradients.

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# target distribution parameters
TRUE_MU, TRUE_SIGMA = 4.0, 3.0

def get_grads(mu, sigma):
    """
    compute the gradients of Loss w.r.t. mu and sigma
    Loss is essentially KL(P_true || P_current)
    """
    # dL/dmu
    grad_mu = (mu - TRUE_MU) / (sigma**2)
    # dL/dsigma
    grad_sigma = (1.0/sigma) - ((TRUE_SIGMA**2 + (TRUE_MU - mu)**2) / (sigma**3))
    return np.array([grad_mu, grad_sigma])

def get_fisher_inverse(sigma):
    """
    the analytic Fisher matrix of a Gaussian is diagonal:
    F = diag(1/sigma^2, 2/sigma^2)
    F_inv = diag(sigma^2, 0.5*sigma^2)
    """
    return np.array([sigma**2, 0.5 * sigma**2]) # return the diagonal elements

def optimize(opt_type, steps=50, lr=0.1):
    mu, sigma = -2.0, 0.5 # starting point: far away, and sigma small (high-curvature region)
    path = [[mu, sigma]]
    
    for _ in range(steps):
        grad = get_grads(mu, sigma)
        
        if opt_type == 'SGD':
            # ordinary gradient: directly subtract the gradient
            update = -lr * grad
        elif opt_type == 'Natural':
            # natural gradient: multiply by F_inv
            F_inv = get_fisher_inverse(sigma)
            update = -lr * (grad * F_inv) # elementwise multiplication equals matrix multiplication (diagonal)
            
        mu += update[0]
        sigma += update[1]
        sigma = max(sigma, 0.05) # keep numerical stability
        path.append([mu, sigma])
        
    return np.array(path)

# --- visualization ---
sgd_path = optimize('SGD', lr=0.05) # SGD needs a tiny learning rate or it diverges
nat_path = optimize('Natural', lr=0.2)

# plot the loss contour
mu_range = np.linspace(-3, 5, 50)
sigma_range = np.linspace(0.1, 4, 50)
M, S = np.meshgrid(mu_range, sigma_range)
# simplified loss visualization
Loss = np.log(S) + (TRUE_SIGMA**2 + (TRUE_MU - M)**2)/(2*S**2)

plt.figure(figsize=(10, 6))
plt.contour(M, S, Loss, levels=20, cmap='gray', alpha=0.4)
plt.plot(TRUE_MU, TRUE_SIGMA, 'k*', markersize=15, label='Optimal')

# plot the paths
plt.plot(sgd_path[:,0], sgd_path[:,1], 'r.-', linewidth=2, label='SGD (Euclidean Gradient)')
plt.plot(nat_path[:,0], nat_path[:,1], 'g.-', linewidth=2, label='Natural Gradient')

# add an arrow explanation
plt.annotate('High Curvature Zone', xy=(-2, 0.5), xytext=(-1, 1.5),
             arrowprops=dict(facecolor='black', shrink=0.05))

plt.xlabel('Mean ($\mu$)')
plt.ylabel('Std ($\sigma$)')
plt.title('Optimization Trajectory: The Impact of Geometry')
plt.legend()
plt.grid(True, alpha=0.3)
plt.show()
```

### 5.2 Analysis of the Phenomenon

![optimize_trajectory](../images/OptimizationTrajectory.png)


This figure perfectly demonstrates Riemannian geometry's dominance over optimization:

1.  **SGD's blind sprint (red line)**
    *   **Phenomenon**: starting from $\sigma=0.5$, it instantly launches vertically to $\sigma \approx 18$, completely missing the target.
    *   **Reason**: gradient explosion. In the small-$\sigma$ region, the gradient's denominator contains a $\sigma^3$ term ($\nabla_\sigma L \approx \dots / \sigma^3$). SGD is fooled by the huge gradient value and takes an oversized step, causing violent parameter oscillation.
    *   **Essence**: a classic case of **step size not matching the terrain**.

2.  **Natural gradient's sensible navigation (green line)**
    *   **Phenomenon**: heads straight to the finish along an elegant arc, no oscillation.
    *   **Reason**: curvature correction. $\mathbf{F}^{-1}$ here is proportional to $\sigma^2$. Acting as a damper, it cancels the gradient explosion:
        $$ \text{Update} \propto \mathbf{F}^{-1} \cdot \nabla L \approx \sigma^2 \cdot \frac{1}{\sigma^3} = \frac{1}{\sigma} $$
    *   **Essence**: it automatically recognizes being in a `high-curvature sensitive region` and proactively shrinks the step size.

**Conclusion**:
This is why large-model training can't do without Adam. By dividing by the root-mean-square of gradients ($\sqrt{v_t}$), Adam essentially simulates natural gradient's **adaptive scaling mechanism**, preventing the model from collapsing in parameter-sensitive (high-curvature) regions.

## 6. Summary

|Dimension|Ordinary Gradient Descent (SGD)|Natural Gradient Descent (NGD)
|--|--|--|
|View|Walking while looking at a map|Walking while looking at a globe
|Assumption|Parameter space is flat (Euclidean)|Parameter space is curved (Riemannian)
|Formula|$\Delta \theta = -\eta \nabla L $|$\Delta \theta = -\eta \mathbf{F}^{-1} \nabla L $|
|Drawback|Oscillates in high-curvature regions (like cliffs)|Computing $\mathbf{F}^{-1}$ is extremely expensive
|Modern approximation|Momentum|Adam, K-FAC


Core insight:

Adam became the default optimizer not just because it adjusts the learning rate, but because it's a low-cost approximation of the natural gradient. It lets us approximately walk geodesics even without knowing the space's curvature.
