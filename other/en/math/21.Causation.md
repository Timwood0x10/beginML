# Causal Inference: Structural Equations and Do-Calculus

**Abstract**: This chapter builds an operational system distinct from classical probability theory — **Do-Calculus**. We'll prove that, without assumptions about the graph structure, $P(Y|X)$ cannot reveal physical mechanisms; by introducing the structural causal model (SCM), we can convert the interventional distribution $P(Y|do(X))$ and the counterfactual distribution $P(Y_{x'}|x,y)$ into computable statistical quantities.

---

## 1. Core Definitions and Axioms

### 1.1 The Structural Causal Model (SCM)

Define an SCM as a four-tuple $\mathcal{M} = \langle U, V, F, P(U) \rangle$:

* **$U$ (exogenous variables)**: unobserved background variables (noise), following the joint distribution $P(U)$.
* **$V$ (endogenous variables)**: observed variables in the model $\{V_1, \dots, V_n\}$.
* **$F$ (structural equations)**: a set of functions $v_i = f_i(pa_i, u_i)$, where $pa_i \subseteq V \setminus \{v_i\}$ are the parents of $v_i$. This represents the **physical generative mechanism**.

### 1.2 Bayesian Network Factorization (The Markov Property)

In the un-intervened state, the joint probability distribution $P(v)$ is determined by the graph structure $G$:

$$
P(v_1, \dots, v_n) = \prod_{i=1}^n P(v_i | pa_i)
$$

### 1.3 The Do-Operator

The intervention $do(X=x)$ is defined as **Graph Surgery**:
replace the structural equation $X = f_X(pa_X, u_X)$ with the constant equation $X = x$.
This leads to the **Truncated Factorization**:

$$
P(v_1, \dots, v_n | do(X=x)) = \left. \prod_{i, V_i \neq X} P(v_i | pa_i) \right|_{X=x}
$$

**Corollary**: the intervention changes $X$'s generative mechanism but preserves the physical laws of the rest of the system $P(v_i|pa_i)$.

## 2. The Trap of Statistics: The Mathematical Essence of Simpson's Paradox

Simpson's paradox appears when the overall correlation and the group-wise correlation have opposite signs. Its mathematical essence is **Selection Bias**.

### 2.1 Analytical Derivation of the Bias

Let $T \in \{0,1\}$ be the treatment, $Y$ the outcome, and $Z$ a confounding variable ($Z \to T, Z \to Y$).
We focus on the **Average Treatment Effect (ATE)**:

$$
\tau = \mathbb{E}[Y | do(T=1)] - \mathbb{E}[Y | do(T=0)]
$$

In observational data, the difference is:

$$
\Delta_{obs} = \mathbb{E}[Y | T=1] - \mathbb{E}[Y | T=0]
$$

Using the law of total expectation $\mathbb{E}[Y|T] = \sum_z \mathbb{E}[Y|T,z]P(z|T)$ to expand $\Delta_{obs}$:

$$
\Delta_{obs} = \sum_z \mathbb{E}[Y|T=1, z]P(z|T=1) - \sum_z \mathbb{E}[Y|T=0, z]P(z|T=0)
$$

Assuming a homogeneous effect, i.e., within each group $z$ the effect is the same: $\mathbb{E}[Y|T=1,z] - \mathbb{E}[Y|T=0,z] = \delta$.
Then the observed difference contains two parts:

$$
\Delta_{obs} = \underbrace{\delta}_{\text{true effect}} + \underbrace{\sum_z \mathbb{E}[Y|T=0,z] \left[ P(z|T=1) - P(z|T=0) \right]}_{\text{selection bias (Bias)}}
$$

### 2.2 Conclusion

* If $P(z|T=1) \neq P(z|T=0)$ (i.e., $T$ and $Z$ are correlated), then $\Delta_{obs} \neq \delta$.
* **The core of causal inference** is to force $P(z|do(T)) = P(z)$ through the $do(T)$ operation, thereby eliminating the bias term above.

## 3. Causal Identification: The Back-door Adjustment Formula

How do we convert the unobservable $P(Y|do(X))$ into observable probabilities $P(Y|X, Z)$?

### 3.1 The Identification Theorem

If the variable set $Z$ satisfies the **Back-door Criterion**:

1. $Z$ contains no descendants of $X$.
2. $Z$ blocks every path between $X$ and $Y$ that contains an arrow pointing into $X$ ($X \leftarrow \dots$).

Then we have the **Adjustment Formula**:

$$
P(Y=y | do(X=x)) = \sum_z P(Y=y | X=x, Z=z) P(Z=z)
$$

### 3.2 Proof Sketch

Based on the truncated factorization:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(y | do(x)) &= \sum_z P(y, z | do(x)) \\
&= \sum_z P(y | x, z, do(x)) P(z | do(x)) \\
\end{aligned}
$$

Since $Z$ satisfies the back-door criterion:

1. $Z$ is upstream of $X$ and unaffected by $do(X)$ $\Rightarrow P(z | do(x)) = P(z)$.
2. Given $Z, X$, the mechanism for $Y$ is unchanged $\Rightarrow P(y | x, z, do(x)) = P(y | x, z)$ (modularity).
   QED.

## 4. Counterfactual Reasoning

Counterfactuals sit on the third rung of Pearl's causal ladder, involving the symbol $P(Y_{x'} | X=x, Y=y)$.
**Physical meaning**: given that in the real world $X=x$ caused $Y=y$, if we turn back time and force $X=x'$, keeping all other conditions (the exogenous variables $U$) unchanged, what would $Y$ be?

### 4.1 Abduction-Action-Prediction

This is a rigorous computational procedure:

1. **Abduction**: compute the posterior distribution of the exogenous variables.

   $$
   P(U | x, y) = \frac{P(y|x, U) P(x|U) P(U)}{\int P(y|x, u) P(x|u) P(u) du}
   $$

   *Note: this is essentially inferring a specific individual's intrinsic attributes (like genes, talents).*
2. **Action**: build the new model $\mathcal{M}_{x'}$, replacing the equation with $X=x'$, but **preserving the distribution of $U$**.
3. **Prediction**: compute the expectation of $Y$ on the new model.

   $$
   \mathbb{E}[Y_{x'} | X=x, Y=y] = \int f_Y(x', pa_Y, u) \cdot P(u | x, y) du
   $$

### 4.2 Connection to Deep Learning

In causal VAEs or generative models, we usually can't compute the integral exactly; instead we approximate it with Variational Inference:

$$
P(u | x, y) \approx q_\phi(u | x, y)
$$

This is why generative models (Ch 15) are the necessary path to counterfactual reasoning.

## 5. Deep Causal Learning: Independent Causal Mechanisms (ICM)

Bringing causal ideas into deep neural networks centers on finding representations robust to environmental changes. This connects causal inference with generalization theory (Ch 7, 9).

### 5.1 The ICM Principle

The joint distribution of the physical world

$$
P(X_1, \ldots, X_n)
$$

can be decomposed into a product of independent mechanisms:

$$
P(s) = \prod_{i=1}^n P(X_i | P A_i)
$$

The ICM assumption: the physical mechanisms $P(X_i | P A_i)$ are independent of each other and contain no information about one another. This means that when a distribution shift occurs, usually only a sparse few mechanisms change while the others remain invariant.

### 5.2 Sparse Mechanism Shift

In deep learning, if we learn the correct causal variables (Disentangled Representation), then migrating from the training set (source domain $e_{train}$) to the test set (target domain $e_{test}$) should require minimal parameter changes.
We can define a loss function to find these causal modules. Suppose the neural network is decomposed into $k$ modules ${m_1,\ldots,m_k}$; under different environments $e$:

$$
L_{\text{Total}} = \underbrace{\sum_{e} L_{\text{pred}}(f(x), y; e)}_{\text{prediction error}} + \lambda \underbrace{\sum_{j=1}^{k} \left\|\nabla_{e} P(m_j \mid e)\right\|}_{\text{mechanism-change penalty}}
$$

Converted into KL divergence form:

$$
\min_{\theta} \sum_{i} D_{\text{KL}}\left(P(M_i^{\text{train}}) \parallel P(M_i^{\text{test}})\right)
$$

This is exactly the mathematical foundation of Invariant Risk Minimization (IRM) and Bengio's Consciousness Prior.

## 6. Code Practice: Verifying Do-Calculus

We'll build an SCM matching the structure $Z \to T, Z \to Y, T \to Y$ and verify the correctness of the adjustment formula.

```python
import networkx as nx
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import dowhy
from dowhy import CausalModel
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# ==========================================
# 1. define the physical world (SCM)
# ==========================================
class StructuralCausalModel:
    def __init__(self, N=10000):
        np.random.seed(42)
        self.N = N
  
    def generate(self):
        # U_Z ~ N(0,1)
        self.Z = np.random.normal(0, 1, self.N)
      
        # T = f_T(Z, U_T), T is discrete (0/1)
        # logit mechanism: the larger Z, the higher the probability of T=1
        logit = 0.8 * self.Z + np.random.normal(0, 1, self.N)
        self.T = (logit > 0).astype(int)
      
        # Y = f_Y(T, Z, U_Y)
        # true causal coefficient: T -> Y = 2.0
        # confounding coefficient: Z -> Y = 1.5
        self.Y = 2.0 * self.T + 1.5 * self.Z + np.random.normal(0, 1, self.N)
      
        return pd.DataFrame({'Z': self.Z, 'T': self.T, 'Y': self.Y})

scm = StructuralCausalModel()
df = scm.generate()

# ==========================================
# 2. causal inference (DoWhy framework)
# ==========================================

# A. define the graph structure (assume we know the qualitative relations, not the quantitative coefficients)
causal_graph = """
digraph {
    Z -> T;
    Z -> Y;
    T -> Y;
}
"""

model = CausalModel(
    data=df,
    treatment='T',
    outcome='Y',
    graph=causal_graph
)

# B. identify the estimand (apply the back-door criterion)
identified_estimand = model.identify_effect()

# C. estimate (apply the adjustment formula)
# use linear regression as the statistical adjustment: E[Y|T,Z]
causal_estimate = model.estimate_effect(
    identified_estimand,
    method_name="backdoor.linear_regression"
)

# D. naive estimate (direct observation)
naive_effect = df[df['T']==1]['Y'].mean() - df[df['T']==0]['Y'].mean()

# E. refutation test
refute = model.refute_estimate(
    identified_estimand,
    causal_estimate,
    method_name="placebo_treatment_refuter"
)

# ==========================================
# 3. visualization and verification
# ==========================================

# 1. draw the DAG (using a canonical layout)
plt.figure(figsize=(5, 4))
G = nx.DiGraph()
G.add_edges_from([('Z', 'T'), ('Z', 'Y'), ('T', 'Y')])
pos = {'Z': (0, 1), 'T': (-1, 0), 'Y': (1, 0)} # inverted triangle layout

nx.draw(G, pos, with_labels=True, node_color='lightblue', 
        node_size=3000, arrowsize=20, font_size=12, font_weight='bold', 
        edge_color='black')
plt.title("Causal DAG Topology")
plt.show()

# 2. bias comparison chart
results = {
    'Naive Correlation': naive_effect,
    'Causal Estimate': causal_estimate.value,
    'Ground Truth': 2.0,
    'Placebo Refutation': refute.new_effect
}

print(f"{'Metric':<20} | {'Value':<10}")
print("-" * 35)
for k, v in results.items():
    print(f"{k:<20} | {v:.4f}")

plt.figure(figsize=(8, 5))
colors = ['red', 'green', 'blue', 'gray']
bars = plt.bar(results.keys(), results.values(), color=colors, alpha=0.7, edgecolor='black')
plt.axhline(2.0, color='blue', linestyle='--', label='True Effect (2.0)')
plt.ylabel('Effect Size')
plt.title('Correcting Selection Bias with Do-Calculus')
plt.legend()

for bar in bars:
    height = bar.get_height()
    plt.text(bar.get_x() + bar.get_width()/2., height,
             f'{height:.2f}', ha='center', va='bottom')

plt.show()
```

### Summary: From Graph-Theoretic Assumptions to Statistical Truth

In this chapter we crossed the gap between `correlation` and `causality` in machine learning. This crossing isn't philosophical speculation; it's built on rigorous mathematical derivation.

### The Mathematical Unification of Three Levels

We used a unified symbolic system to describe the three rungs of human cognition:

| Rung | Core question | Mathematical expression | Operational essence | Data requirement |
| :------------------ | :-------------------------------- | :---------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | :----------------------------- |
| **L1 Association** | "When we see $X$, what is $Y$?" | $P(Y \mid X)$ | **Bayesian update**`<br>`update the posterior based on observed evidence. | observational data $D$ |
| **L2 Intervention** | "If we do $X$, will $Y$ change?" | $P(Y \mid do(X))$ | **Graph surgery & truncated factorization**`<br>`cut the parent edges pointing into $X$, force the assignment. | $D$ + causal graph $G$ |
| **L3 Counterfactual** | "If we hadn't done $X$, what would $Y$ be?" | $P(Y_{x'} \mid x, y)$ | **Abduction-action-prediction**`<br>`infer the exogenous variables $U$ (Abduction) $\to$ parallel-universe intervention. | $D$ + $G$ + structural equations $F$ |
