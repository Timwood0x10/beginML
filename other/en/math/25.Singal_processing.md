# Signal Processing & Time-Frequency Analysis

**Abstract**: Machine learning data sources are often samplings of the physical world: sound, stock market fluctuations, brain waves (EEG). These signals are essentially **waves**. Classic deep learning (like MLP) handles static vectors; RNNs handle discrete sequences — but they often ignore the **frequency-domain characteristics** of signals. This chapter starts from the **Heisenberg uncertainty principle**, explores the limitations of the Fourier transform, introduces the short-time Fourier transform (STFT) and the wavelet transform (Wavelet), and dives into the continuous-time system discretization math behind the current large-model favorite **Mamba (SSM)**.

---

## 1. The Limitations of the Fourier Transform and STFT

### 1.1 From Global to Local

The standard **Fourier Transform (FT)** converts a time-domain signal $x(t)$ into a frequency-domain signal $X(\omega)$:

$$
X(\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t) e^{-j\omega t} dt
$$

**Fatal flaw**: the integration range is $(-\infty, \infty)$. It tells you "what frequencies are in the signal," but not "when they happen."

* Example: a recording of "piano first, then violin" and a recording of "piano and violin playing simultaneously" may have very similar global spectra.

### 1.2 The Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT)

To introduce the time dimension, we add a sliding **Window Function** $w(t)$ (like a Hann window) to the signal:

$$
STFT(t, \omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(\tau) w(\tau - t) e^{-j\omega \tau} d\tau
$$

This produces a 2D complex matrix, whose squared magnitude $|STFT(t, \omega)|^2$ is the **Spectrogram**.

* **Mathematical essence**: a spectrogram is an image (matrix) — this is why CNNs can be directly applied to audio classification tasks.

### 1.3 The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle

Why can't we have both perfect time resolution and perfect frequency resolution? This isn't a technical limitation; it's a fundamental mathematical truth.

Define the time spread $\sigma_t$ and the frequency spread $\sigma_\omega$. For any nonzero function $f(t)$:

$$
\sigma_t \cdot \sigma_\omega \ge \frac{1}{2}
$$

* **Narrow window** (small $\sigma_t$): precise time localization, but the frequency domain gets convolution-blurred, so frequency resolution is poor (large $\sigma_\omega$).
* **Wide window** (large $\sigma_t$): precise frequency localization, but time is blurred — you don't know when transients occur.
* **Gabor transform**: when the window function is a Gaussian, equality holds (reaching the theoretical limit).

## 2. The Wavelet Transform

The STFT's drawback is its fixed window size. Yet real signals usually contain **high-frequency transients** (needing high time resolution) and **low-frequency backgrounds** (needing high frequency resolution).

### 2.1 Multi-Resolution Analysis (MRA)

The wavelet transform no longer uses sine waves $e^{j\omega t}$ as the basis, but a **wavelet basis** $\psi(t)$.
The Continuous Wavelet Transform (CWT) is defined as:

$$
W(a, b) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{a}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t) \psi^* \left( \frac{t - b}{a} \right) dt
$$

* **Scale factor $a$**: similar to the reciprocal of frequency.
  * Small $a$ $\to$ waveform compressed $\to$ captures **high frequency** $\to$ time window narrows (high time resolution).
  * Large $a$ $\to$ waveform stretched $\to$ captures **low frequency** $\to$ time window widens (high frequency resolution).
* **Shift factor $b$**: controls the time position.

### 2.2 A Mathematical Microscope

The wavelet transform is like a zooming microscope:

* When viewing the low-frequency big picture, the field of view is wide.
* When viewing high-frequency details, it focuses on the micro scale.
  This **time-frequency adaptive** property makes it the best tool for non-stationary signals (like ECG).

## 3. State Space Models (SSM) and Mamba

The core idea of the newest sequence-modeling architectures (like S4, Mamba): **treat the deep learning model as the discretization of a continuous-time system.**

### 3.1 Continuous System (ODE View)

A linear time-invariant (LTI) system is described by differential equations:

$$
\begin{cases}
h'(t) = \mathbf{A}h(t) + \mathbf{B}x(t) & \text{(state equation)} \\
y(t) = \mathbf{C}h(t) & \text{(output equation)}
\end{cases}
$$

where $x(t)$ is the input (like an audio waveform), $h(t)$ is the hidden state, and $\mathbf{A}$ is the evolution matrix.

### 3.2 Discretization

Computers can only handle discrete data. Using Zero-Order Hold (ZOH) or bilinear transform, we can convert the above ODE into a recursive form:

$$
h_k = \mathbf{\bar{A}} h_{k-1} + \mathbf{\bar{B}} x_k
$$

This looks exactly like an RNN!

### 3.3 Convolution-Recurrence Duality

This is the most fascinating mathematical property of SSMs:

1. **Training (parallel)**: unrolling the recurrence, $y$ can be written as a convolution of the input $x$ with the kernel $\mathbf{K}$:
   $$
   y = x * \mathbf{K}, \quad \text{where } \mathbf{K} = (\mathbf{C}\mathbf{B}, \mathbf{C}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}, \mathbf{C}\mathbf{A}^2\mathbf{B}, \dots)
   $$

   This lets training parallelize like a CNN (no need to wait for step $t-1$).
2. **Inference (serial)**: using the recurrence $h_k = \mathbf{\bar{A}} h_{k-1} + \dots$, the inference cost is $O(1)$ (no need to look at the whole cache like a Transformer).

### 3.4 The HiPPO Matrix: The Math of Memory

If $\mathbf{A}$ is randomly initialized, the model forgets history.
**HiPPO (High-order Polynomial Projection Operators)** theory proves that when $\mathbf{A}$ takes a specific matrix form, the hidden state $h(t)$ exactly stores the projection coefficients of the historical input $x(t)$ onto the Legendre polynomial basis.
This mathematically solves the **long-range dependency** problem.

## 4. Neural Audio Mathematics

### 4.1 Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCC)

Human hearing's perception of frequency isn't linear but logarithmic (more sensitive to low frequencies).
The **Mel scale** formula:

$$
M(f) = 2595 \log_{10}(1 + \frac{f}{700})
$$

The MFCC computation pipeline: Fourier transform $\to$ power spectrum $\to$ Mel filter bank $\to$ log $\to$ **Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT)**.

* **DCT's role**: decorrelation. Adjacent frequency bands in the spectrum are highly correlated; DCT transforms them into independent feature coefficients, more suitable for Gaussian mixture models (GMM) or neural networks.

### 4.2 Complex-Valued Neural Networks

In the STFT, the Phase contains important structural information.
The traditional approach takes only the magnitude $|STFT|$ and discards the phase. But in speech enhancement or source separation, phase is crucial.
**Complex convolution**:
Let $W = A + iB, h = x + iy$, then

$$
W * h = (A*x - B*y) + i(B*x + A*y)
$$

Complex networks respect the complex algebraic structure; compared to treating real and imaginary parts as two channels (2-channel real), they have better phase consistency and generalization.

## 5. Code Practice: Visualizing the Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle

We'll generate a **Chirp Signal** whose frequency changes over time, and compare the results of FFT and STFT to intuitively show the time-frequency trade-off.

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.signal import stft, chirp

# ==========================================
# 1. generate the signal: chirp
# ==========================================
fs = 1000          # sampling rate 1000Hz
T = 2.0            # duration 2 seconds
t = np.linspace(0, T, int(T*fs))

# the signal sweeps from 50Hz to 200Hz
# x(t) = cos(2 * pi * (f0 + (f1-f0)/T * t) * t)
signal = chirp(t, f0=50, f1=200, t1=T, method='linear')

# ==========================================
# 2. global Fourier transform (FFT)
# ==========================================
freqs = np.fft.rfftfreq(len(t), d=1/fs)
fft_mag = np.abs(np.fft.rfft(signal))

# ==========================================
# 3. short-time Fourier transform (STFT)
# ==========================================
# nperseg controls the window size, showing the Heisenberg trade-off
nperseg = 64 
f_stft, t_stft, Zxx = stft(signal, fs=fs, nperseg=nperseg)
spectrogram = np.abs(Zxx)

# ==========================================
# 4. visualization comparison
# ==========================================
plt.figure(figsize=(12, 8))

# --- Plot 1: time-domain waveform ---
plt.subplot(3, 1, 1)
plt.plot(t, signal)
plt.title("1. Time Domain: Chirp Signal (50Hz -> 200Hz)")
plt.xlabel("Time (s)")
plt.ylabel("Amplitude")
plt.xlim(0, 0.5) # only look at the first 0.5 seconds of detail

# --- Plot 2: global frequency domain (FFT) ---
plt.subplot(3, 1, 2)
plt.plot(freqs, fft_mag, color='r')
plt.title("2. Frequency Domain (FFT): Loss of Time Information")
plt.xlabel("Frequency (Hz)")
plt.ylabel("Magnitude")
plt.text(100, np.max(fft_mag)*0.8, "We know frequencies 50-200Hz exist,\nbut WHEN do they occur? FFT cannot answer.", fontsize=12, bbox=dict(facecolor='white', alpha=0.8))

# --- Plot 3: time-frequency domain (STFT spectrogram) ---
plt.subplot(3, 1, 3)
plt.pcolormesh(t_stft, f_stft, spectrogram, shading='gouraud', cmap='inferno')
plt.title(f"3. Time-Frequency Domain (STFT): The Trajectory (Window Size={nperseg})")
plt.ylabel("Frequency (Hz)")
plt.xlabel("Time (s)")
plt.colorbar(label="Magnitude")
plt.ylim(0, 300)

plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

Analysis of the experimental results

- Time-domain plot: you can see the sine wave getting denser and denser, intuitively feeling the frequency rising.
- FFT plot: you see a rectangular "plateau" from 50Hz to 200Hz. This tells you the signal contains these frequencies, but completely loses the key information that "the frequency increases linearly over time."
- STFT plot: you see a bright yellow diagonal line. The horizontal axis is time, the vertical axis is frequency. This line clearly depicts the trajectory of frequency evolving over time.
- Heisenberg demo: try modifying `nperseg` (the window size) in the code.
  - If nperseg=16 (narrow window): the line on the time axis will be very thin (accurate time), but it widens and blurs on the frequency axis (inaccurate frequency).
  - If nperseg=256 (wide window): the line on the frequency axis is very thin (accurate frequency), but the time axis blurs.

## 6. Summary

Signal processing isn't just a preprocessing step; it's the key to understanding the physical essence of data.

- The Heisenberg principle tells us we can never achieve infinite precision in both the time and frequency domains simultaneously.
- The wavelet transform uses multi-resolution thinking to balance this trade-off at different scales.
- SSM (Mamba) uses the discretization of continuous systems to unblock the "governor and conception vessels" of CNN (parallel training) and RNN (fast inference), becoming a mathematical weapon for ultra-long sequences.
