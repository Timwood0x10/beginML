## Bayesian Deep Learning and Probabilistic Programming: Quantifying Weight Uncertainty

⚠️ A macroscopic view: the neural network's `third mathematical leap`
Reviewing the AI mathematical edifice we've built, we can see three clear cognitive leaps:

- The first leap (function fitting): we thought of a neural network as a complex function $y=f(x;w)$
. The goal was to find an optimal set of weights $w^*$ (a point estimate) minimizing error. This was the era of SGD and backpropagation.

- The second leap (generative models): we thought of data $x$ as following some distribution $p(x)$
. The goal was to learn this distribution and sample from it. This was the era of GANs, VAEs, and diffusion models.

- The third leap (Bayesian deep learning): we think of the weights $w$ themselves as random variables following some distribution $p(w|D)$
    - Core idea: we can never find the `one unique correct` weights.

    - Goal: no longer finding the peak of a mountain (MLE), but describing the shape of the whole mountain (posterior inference).

This note explores the probabilistic programming math principles common to all deep learning frameworks (TensorFlow, PyTorch, JAX, Julia).

---
## 1. Frequentist vs. Bayesian: A Clash of Worldviews

In deep learning, we usually face two philosophies of parameter estimation:

### 1.1 The Frequentist — Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE)

This is the default view of our previous 16 notes.
Assumption: there exists an objective, true, fixed set of weights $w^*$
. Method: through data $D$, find the point that looks most like $w^*$.

$$
w_{MLE}=argmax_{w}P(D|w)
$$

- Limitation: overconfidence. Even when facing data it has never seen, the model gives a definite prediction, because it doesn't know that it `doesn't know`.

### 1.2 The Bayesian — Posterior Inference

This is the probabilistic programming view.

- Assumption: the weights $w$ are uncertain; they form a probability distribution. We have a prior belief $P(w)$ about them (e.g., believing the weights follow a Gaussian).
Method: after observing data $D$, we update our belief about $w$ to obtain the posterior distribution $P(w|D)$.

$$
p(w|D) = \frac{p(D|w)p(w)}{p(D)}
$$

- Limitation: underconfidence. The computation is intractable. And the result is sensitive to the choice of `prior distribution`.

## 2. The Core Mathematical Difficulty: The Intractable Evidence

Bayes' formula looks simple, but in a neural network it contains a mathematical black hole: the denominator $p(D)$

$$
p(D) = \int p(D|w)p(w)dw
$$

- Physical meaning: this is the Marginal Likelihood, also called Model Evidence. It represents the total probability that the model generates the current data.

- Computational disaster:
    - For simple linear regression, this integral has a closed-form solution.
    - For deep neural networks, the weight $w$ may have a billion dimensions. Integrating a non-convex function over high-dimensional space is mathematically unsolvable exactly (Intractable) at present.

The entire history of probabilistic programming and Bayesian deep learning is a history of `how to get around this integral`.

---

## 3. Two Schools: Monte Carlo MCMC vs. Variational Inference VI

To get around that uncomputable integral, mathematicians and computer scientists proposed two fundamentally different paths. These two paths are common to all frameworks (TF, PyTorch, JAX).

### 3.1 School One: Variational Inference (VI)

Idea: turn the `integral problem` into an `optimization problem`.

Since we can't compute the true posterior $P(w|D)$, we find a simple, known distribution family $q_\theta(w)$ (like a Gaussian), then adjust $\theta$ so that $q_\theta(w)$ resembles $P(w|D)$ as closely as possible.

Mathematical tool: KL Divergence:

Goal: minimize $KL(q_\theta(w) || P(w|D))$. After derivation (see below), this is equivalent to maximizing the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO):
$$
L(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{w \sim q_\theta}\left[\log P(D \mid w)\right] - \text{KL}\left(q_{\theta}(w) \mid\mid P(w)\right)
$$

Intuitive explanation:
- First term: reconstruction error. It requires the sampled weights to fit the data well.

- Second term: regularizer. It requires the approximate distribution $q$ not to deviate too far from the prior $p$.

- Advantage: fast! It can be trained with SGD and backpropagation. It's the mainstream in industrial applications.

- Disadvantage: because $q$ is assumed simple (usually Gaussian), it often underestimates variance.

### 3.2 School Two: Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC)

Idea: turn the `computation problem` into a `sampling problem`.
Since we can't compute the analytic form of the distribution, we design a physical process (a Markov chain) that lets particles wander in this space. As time goes to infinity, the positions where particles appear follow the posterior distribution $p(w|D)$.

Mathematical tool: Hamiltonian Dynamics

To wander efficiently in high-dimensional space, the most advanced algorithms (like HMC and NUTS) introduce the physics concept of `momentum`.

$$
H(w,p) = U(w) + K(p)
$$

Treat the negative log-probability as `potential energy` $U(w)$, and introduce the auxiliary variable `momentum` $p$. Particles don't jump randomly; they glide along equipotential contours.

- Advantage: accurate! In theory it converges to the true posterior; it's the `gold standard`.
- Disadvantage: slow! Extremely expensive; usually hard to apply to large neural networks.

## 4. The Bridge Connecting Math and Engineering: The Reparameterization Trick

This is the key technology that lets deep learning frameworks (whether TF or PyTorch) train probabilistic models.

Problem: if there's a sampling operation $z \sim N(\mu, \sigma)$ in the middle of the network, sampling is random, breaking the computational graph so gradients can't backpropagate.

Solution: peel the randomness to the outside.

We don't sample $z$ directly; we sample a standard noise $\epsilon \sim N(0, 1)$, then set:
$$
z = \mu + \sigma \odot \epsilon
$$

Mathematical meaning:
Now, $z$ becomes a deterministic function of $\mu$ and $\sigma$ (albeit with a noise parameter).
$$
\frac{\partial z}{\partial \mu} = 1, \frac{\partial z}{\partial \sigma} = \epsilon
$$

Gradients can flow! This lets Variational Inference (VI) train like an ordinary neural network, using GPU acceleration and the Adam optimizer.

## 5. Classifying Uncertainty: What Exactly Are We Uncertain About?

From a macroscopic view, probabilistic programming helps us distinguish two essentially different kinds of uncertainty:
### 5.1 Aleatoric Uncertainty


- Source: inherent noise in the data itself (e.g., sensor error, coin randomness).

- Characteristic: can't be eliminated with more data.

- Modeling: introduce a $\sigma$ term into the Loss function (as described in note 9).

### 5.2 Epistemic Uncertainty

- Source: the model `hasn't seen` this kind of data, or the model structure isn't good enough. This is a **blind spot in knowledge**.

- Characteristic: can be eliminated by adding more data.

- Modeling: must use a Bayesian neural network (BNN), making the weights distributions.

Macroscopic conclusion: traditional neural networks can only capture the first kind; only Bayesian deep learning can capture both. This is crucial for autonomous driving (encountering unseen road conditions) and medical diagnosis (encountering rare diseases).

## 6. Macroscopic Ecosystem and Engineering Implementation: A Paradigm Shift in Code

How do the mathematical principles land in engineering? Different deep learning frameworks provide different dialects, but they all do the same thing: **transform neural network layers from "deterministic computation" into "probabilistic sampling."**

By comparing the three most mainstream ecosystems, let's see what this "qualitative change of one line of code" actually involves.

### 6.0 Baseline: A Traditional Deterministic Network (Standard PyTorch)
In traditional deep learning, weights are fixed Parameters.

```python
# y = x @ w + b
# on every forward pass, w and b are fixed numbers
layer = nn.Linear(in_features=10, out_features=1)
```


### 6.1 The TensorFlow Ecosystem (TFP - TensorFlow Probability)

Positioning: industrial-grade, made by Google, deeply integrated with Keras.

Features: highly encapsulated API, TPU acceleration support, suited to ultra-large models.
The qualitative change in code:

TFP provides `Probabilistic Layers` that automatically handle the reparameterization trick and KL divergence computation.

```python
import tensorflow_probability as tfp
tfd = tfp.distributions

# define the layer: weights are no longer variables, but random variables following a normal distribution
# Flipout is an efficient Monte Carlo estimation trick
layer = tfp.layers.DenseFlipout(
    units=1,
    kernel_prior_fn=tfp.layers.default_multivariate_normal_fn, # prior P(w)
    kernel_posterior_fn=tfp.layers.default_mean_field_normal_fn() # variational posterior q(w)
)
# behind this one line, TFP automatically adds the KL divergence term (part of the ELBO) to the Loss
```

### 6.2 The PyTorch Ecosystem (Pyro / GPyTorch)

Positioning: academic-grade, made by Uber, deeply integrated with PyTorch.

Features: the dynamic graph makes writing complex probabilistic models (like models with loops and recursion) very intuitive. Used extensively in academia.

The qualitative change in code:
Pyro doesn't encapsulate layers; it uses the pyro.sample primitive to force developers to explicitly think about the distribution of each parameter.

```python
import pyro
import pyro.distributions as dist

# must explicitly define the distribution of the weights
# 1. define the prior (Prior)
w_dist = dist.Normal(0, 1)
b_dist = dist.Normal(0, 1)

# 2. sample (Sample) - reparameterization happens here
w = pyro.sample("w", w_dist)
b = pyro.sample("b", b_dist)

# 3. compute (Compute)
y = x @ w + b
```


### 6.3 The JAX Ecosystem (NumPyro / Distrax / Optax)

Features: uses JAX's XLA compilation and automatic vectorization (vmap).

Current status: NumPyro's MCMC sampling speed is the industry benchmark (faster than both Pyro and TFP), because HMC/NUTS fit JAX's computation model perfectly.

The qualitative change in code:
The code style is functional and stateless.

```python
import numpyro
import numpyro.distributions as dist
import jax.numpy as jnp

def model(x, y=None):
    # syntax similar to Pyro, but runs on the XLA compiler underneath
    w = numpyro.sample("w", dist.Normal(jnp.zeros((10, 1)), jnp.ones((10, 1))))
    b = numpyro.sample("b", dist.Normal(0., 1.))
    
    # JAX matrix multiplication
    mu = jnp.dot(x, w) + b
    
    # observe the data
    numpyro.sample("obs", dist.Normal(mu, 1.0), obs=y)
```


### 6.4 The Julia Ecosystem (Turing.jl)
Features: syntax closest to mathematical formulas; the first choice in scientific computing.

### 6.5 Conclusion: All Roads Lead to Rome

Although TFP encapsulates Layers, Pyro exposes Sample, and NumPyro optimizes compilation, the mathematical foundation behind them is eternal:

1. ELBO: they all try to maximize the evidence lower bound.

2. Reparameterization: they all peel randomness into $\epsilon$, letting gradients flow.

3. KL Divergence: they all use KL divergence to constrain the posterior from deviating from the prior.

Tools will change, but the Bayesian idea of **treating weights as distributions** is the shared soul of all these frameworks.
