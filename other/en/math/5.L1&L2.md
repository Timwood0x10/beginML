**The essence of regularization is doing constrained optimization, and Lagrange multipliers are the master key to this class of problems.**

---

## 🔑 Core Insight: Regularization = The Lagrange Form of Constrained Optimization

### One Sentence to Get It

> **`Adding a regularizer` after the loss function is equivalent to `minimizing the loss under a constraint`.**
> The constraint is simply `the weights must not be too large`.

![L1&L2](../images/l1_vs_l2_regularization.gif)

- On the left, L1 (Lasso):
  Notice what happens when the red point (the parameters) moves close to the $w_1$ axis (horizontal) or the $w_2$ axis (vertical).
  It suddenly gets "sucked onto" the axis like hitting a wall, then slides along the axis toward the center.
  Mathematical meaning: one of the weights has become 0. This is the geometric process of Feature Selection. Because the diamond's "corners" stick out, it's easier for the contour lines to touch them first.
- On the right, L2 (Ridge):
  The blue point spirals down smoothly.
  It gets very close to the origin $(0,0)$, but almost never lands exactly on an axis.
  Mathematical meaning: it just squashes the weights very small (Weight Decay) to prevent overfitting, but doesn't discard features. Because the circular boundary is smooth.

## 🎯 Step 1: Two Equivalent Problem Formulations

### 1.1 Constrained Optimization Form (the primal problem)

**L2 regularization (constraint form)**:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\min_{\mathbf{w}} \quad & \text{Loss}(\mathbf{w}) \\
\text{s.t.} \quad & \|\mathbf{w}\|_2^2 \le C
\end{aligned}
$$

**L1 regularization (constraint form)**:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\min_{\mathbf{w}} \quad & \text{Loss}(\mathbf{w}) \\
\text{s.t.} \quad & \|\mathbf{w}\|_1 \le C
\end{aligned}
$$

**Intuition**:

- Goal: minimize training error
- Constraint: the "total energy" of the weights (L2 norm or L1 norm) must not exceed budget C
- Like shopping (minimize spending) but you only have C dollars in your wallet

---

### 1.2 The Lagrange Form (penalty form)

By Lagrange multipliers, we `stuff` the constraint into the objective:

**L2 regularization (Lagrange form)**:

$$
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}, \lambda) = \text{Loss}(\mathbf{w}) + \lambda \|\mathbf{w}\|_2^2
$$

**L1 regularization (Lagrange form)**:

$$
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}, \lambda) = \text{Loss}(\mathbf{w}) + \lambda \|\mathbf{w}\|_1
$$

**Key point**:

- **λ (the Lagrange multiplier) = the regularization coefficient**
- Bigger λ → tighter constraint → weights squashed smaller
- Smaller λ → looser constraint → close to unconstrained optimization

---

## 🌈 Step 2: A Life Analogy (Making the Correspondence Clear)

### 2.1 The Grocery-Shopping Example

**Scenario**: you're buying fruit at the supermarket (training a model) and want the most satisfying purchase (minimizing loss).

#### Constraint form (primal problem)

> The boss says: "You only have 100 dollars in your wallet (constraint $\|\mathbf{w}\| \le C$). Buy the best fruit within this budget."

- You must choose within the `budget circle` (the feasible region of the constraint)
- The fruit you really want may be outside the circle, but you can't afford it

#### Lagrange form (penalty form)

> The boss says: "You can buy whatever you want, but for every dollar you overspend, I fine you λ dollars (the penalty term $\lambda \|\mathbf{w}\|$)."

- If λ is large (heavy fine), you won't dare overspend → equivalent to a tight constraint
- If λ is small (light fine), you might overspend a bit → equivalent to a loose constraint
- When you find the optimal solution, **the total fine = Lagrange multiplier × overspend amount**

---

### 2.2 The Engineer's Example

**Scenario**: designing a car

#### Constraint form

> "Total weight must not exceed 2 tons (constraint); under that, make the car go as fast as possible (minimize loss)."

#### Lagrange form

> "The car can be any weight, but for every extra kilogram, fuel consumption rises by $\lambda$ liters (penalty). Your goal is to minimize 'speed loss + fuel cost'."

## 📐 Step 3: Deriving Regularization from Lagrange

### 3.1 L2 Regularization as an Example (Full Derivation)

**Constraint problem** (original form):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\min_{\mathbf{w}} \quad & \text{Loss}(\mathbf{w}) \\
\text{s.t.} \quad & \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{w} \le C
\end{aligned}
$$

**Lagrange function** (introduce multiplier λ ≥ 0):

$$
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}, \lambda) = \text{Loss}(\mathbf{w}) + \lambda(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{w} - C)
$$

**KKT conditions**:

1. **Primal feasibility**: $(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{w} \le C)$
2. **Dual feasibility**: $(\lambda \ge 0)$
3. **Complementary slackness**: $(\lambda(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{w} - C) = 0)$
   - Either the constraint is tight ($\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{w} = C$)
   - Or the multiplier is 0 ($\lambda = 0$)
4. **Zero gradient**:
   $$
   \nabla_{\mathbf{w}} \mathcal{L} = \nabla_{\mathbf{w}} \text{Loss}(\mathbf{w}) + 2\lambda \mathbf{w} = 0
   $$

**Ignoring the constant term -λC** (it doesn't affect optimization):

$$
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}, \lambda) = \text{Loss}(\mathbf{w}) + \lambda \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{w}
$$

This is exactly the **L2 regularization form** we know!

**Key understanding**:

- **λ is the "shadow price" of the constraint**
- λ tells us: "if we relax the constraint C a little, how much would the loss drop"
- In practice, we tune λ directly rather than C (because λ is more intuitive)

---

### 3.2 Deriving L1 Regularization

**Constraint problem**:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\min_{\mathbf{w}} \quad & \text{Loss}(\mathbf{w}) \\
\text{s.t.} \quad & \sum_{i=1}^{n} |w_i| \le C
\end{aligned}
$$

**Lagrange function**:

$$
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}, \lambda) = \text{Loss}(\mathbf{w}) + \lambda\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n} |w_i| - C\right)
$$

**Ignoring the constant -λC**:

$$
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}, \lambda) = \text{Loss}(\mathbf{w}) + \lambda \sum_{i=1}^{n} |w_i|
$$

This is exactly the **L1 regularization form**!

---

## 🎨 Step 4: Geometric Intuition (Why Does L1 Produce Sparse Solutions?)

### 4.1 The Shape of the Constraint Region

**L2 constraint** ($\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{w} \le C$):

- Shape: circle (2D) / sphere (high-dimensional)
- Feature: smooth, no corners

**L1 constraint** ($\|\mathbf{w}\|_1 \le C$):

- Shape: diamond (2D) / polyhedron (high-dimensional)
- Feature: has corners (on the coordinate axes)

### 4.2 Visualization (2D case)

```
L2 constraint (circle)          L1 constraint (diamond)
     w2                          w2
      ↑                           ↑
      |    ○○○                    |    ◇
      |   ○   ○                   |   / \
      |  ○     ○                  |  /   \
------+○-------○→ w1       -------+-------◇→ w1
      |  ○     ○                  |  \   /
      |   ○   ○                   |   \ /
      |    ○○○                    |    ◇
```

**Blue contour lines**: the loss function's level sets (ellipses)
**Red region**: the constraint's feasible region

### 4.3 Where Is the Optimum?

**The optimum = the first contact point between a contour line and the constraint boundary**

#### L2 regularization:

- A contour line is tangent to the circular boundary
- The tangent point is usually not on an axis
- **All weights $w_i$ are nonzero** (just squashed smaller)

#### L1 regularization:

- A contour line intersects the diamond boundary
- Because the diamond has corners (on the axes), **the contour easily hits a corner first**
- At a corner, some coordinates are 0
- **Produces a sparse solution** (some w_i = 0)

#### The descent trajectory `slide effect`

- **L2's descent path**: like a ball rolling toward the bottom of a bowl. If it overshoots, it turns smoothly and spirals toward the origin, but it's hard to stop exactly on an axis.
- **L1's descent path**:
  - When a weight is large, it's driven by both the gradient and the fixed penalty
  - Once a weight $w_i$ shrinks and hits 0 (an axis), a miracle happens:
    - If at this point the `loss gradient` is too weak to overcome the `regularization penalty` ($|\nabla \text{Loss}|<\lambda$), the weight gets `stuck` at 0 and slides along the axis without turning back nonzero.
    - This is the dynamical reason L1 produces sparse solutions — the axis has `stickiness`!

### 4.4 The Math: Why Can L1 Drive Weights to Zero?

Take a single weight $(w_i)$ and its gradient update:

**L2 regularization**:

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial w_i}\left[\text{Loss} + \lambda w_i^2\right] = \frac{\partial \text{Loss}}{\partial w_i} + 2\lambda w_i
$$

- Gradient contribution: $(2\lambda w_i)$ (proportional to $(w_i)$)
- When $(w_i)$ is small, the penalty is also small
- **$(w_i)$ tends toward a small value but never reaches 0**

**L1 regularization**:

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial w_i}\left[\text{Loss} + \lambda |w_i|\right] = \frac{\partial \text{Loss}}{\partial w_i} + \lambda \cdot \text{sign}(w_i)
$$

- Gradient contribution: $(\lambda \cdot \text{sign}(w_i))$ (a constant!)
- **No matter how small $(w_i)$ is, there's a fixed penalty $\lambda$**
- When the loss gradient is smaller than $\lambda$, $(w_i)$ gets pushed straight to 0

**Analogy**:

- L2: a rubber band (pull proportional to distance) — the closer to 0, the weaker the pull
- L1: a magnet (fixed attraction) — as long as you're near 0, you get pulled in

## 🔬 Step 5: The Correspondence Between λ and C

### 5.1 Theoretical Relationship

For a given constraint C, there's a unique λ that makes the two forms equivalent:

**Strong duality condition** (convex optimization + Slater's condition):

$$
\min_{\mathbf{w}: \|\mathbf{w}\| \le C} \text{Loss}(\mathbf{w}) 
= \min_{\mathbf{w}} \left[\text{Loss}(\mathbf{w}) + \lambda \|\mathbf{w}\|\right]
$$

**Complementary slackness tells us**:

$$
\lambda^* \cdot (\|\mathbf{w}^*\| - C) = 0
$$

**Three cases**:

| Case | Constraint | Value of $\lambda$ | Explanation |
| ------------ | ------------------------ | ------------------------ | ------------ |
| Constraint inactive | $\|\mathbf{w}^*\| < C$ | $\lambda^* = 0$ | No regularization needed |
| Constraint exactly saturated | $\|\mathbf{w}^*\| = C$ | $\lambda^* > 0$ | The constraint is exactly tight |
| Constraint too tight | no feasible solution | $\lambda^* \to \infty$ | C too small |

### 5.2 How to Tune in Practice?

**Constraint form** (tune C):

```python
# need to manually search the range of C (not intuitive)
for C in [0.1, 1.0, 10.0, 100.0]:
    model = train_with_constraint(C)
```

**Lagrange form** (tune λ):

```python
# λ is more intuitive: the bigger the value, the stronger the regularization
for lambda_reg in [0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1.0]:
    model = train_with_regularization(lambda_reg)
```

**Why is tuning λ more common?**

- λ's physical meaning is clear: `penalty strength`
- C's range is hard to estimate (depends on the weight scale)
- λ can be tuned continuously from 0 (0 = no regularization)

## 💻 Step 6: Code Demo (Seeing Lagrange)

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.optimize import minimize

# generate data
np.random.seed(42)
X = np.random.randn(50, 2)
y = 3*X[:, 0] - 2*X[:, 1] + 0.5*np.random.randn(50)

# loss function (mean squared error)
def loss(w, X, y):
    return np.mean((X @ w - y)**2)

# constraint-form optimization (L2)
def optimize_constrained(X, y, C):
    """constraint form: min Loss(w)  s.t. ||w||^2 <= C"""
    def objective(w):
        return loss(w, X, y)
  
    def constraint(w):
        return C - np.sum(w**2)  # must be >= 0
  
    cons = {'type': 'ineq', 'fun': constraint}
    result = minimize(objective, x0=[0, 0], constraints=cons)
    return result.x

# Lagrange-form optimization (L2)
def optimize_regularized(X, y, lambda_reg):
    """Lagrange form: min Loss(w) + λ||w||^2"""
    def objective(w):
        return loss(w, X, y) + lambda_reg * np.sum(w**2)
  
    result = minimize(objective, x0=[0, 0])
    return result.x

# test different C and λ
C_values = [0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 5.0]
lambda_values = [2.0, 1.0, 0.5, 0.2]

print("Constraint form (given C):")
for C in C_values:
    w = optimize_constrained(X, y, C)
    print(f"C={C:.1f}: w={w}, ||w||²={np.sum(w**2):.3f}")

print("\nLagrange form (given λ):")
for lam in lambda_values:
    w = optimize_regularized(X, y, lam)
    print(f"λ={lam:.1f}: w={w}, ||w||²={np.sum(w**2):.3f}")
```

| **When to use** | prevent overfitting, handle collinearity | feature selection, sparse modeling |

## Analyzing L1 & L2 Regularization from the `Weight Decay` Angle

Analyzing L1 and L2 regularization from the **weight decay** angle lets you truly understand their essential difference during parameter updates. This is more intuitive than just looking at the loss function, because it directly reveals **how the weights shrink step by step**.

---

## 🔍 **Core Insight: Weight Decay = "Automatic Slimming" During Parameter Updates**

In gradient descent, weight decay makes each update multiply the weights by a coefficient slightly less than 1, achieving "automatic slimming".

### Basic gradient descent formula:

$$
\mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} - \eta \nabla \text{Loss}(\mathbf{w})
$$

### Update with weight decay:

$$
\mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} - \eta \nabla \text{Loss}(\mathbf{w}) - \eta \lambda \mathbf{w}
$$

This is equivalent to:

$$
\mathbf{w} \leftarrow (1 - \eta \lambda) \mathbf{w} - \eta \nabla \text{Loss}(\mathbf{w})
$$

**Key**: $(1 - \eta \lambda)$ is a number with **0 < coefficient < 1**, so every update makes the weights "shrink" a little.

---

## 📊 **1. L2 Weight Decay: Smooth `Multiplicative Slimming`**

### Deriving the update formula

For L2 regularization, the loss function is:

$$
\text{TotalLoss} = \text{Loss}(\mathbf{w}) + \frac{\lambda}{2} \|\mathbf{w}\|_2^2
$$

Gradient:

$$
\nabla \text{TotalLoss} = \nabla \text{Loss}(\mathbf{w}) + \lambda \mathbf{w}
$$

**Weight update**:

$$
\mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} - \eta (\nabla \text{Loss}(\mathbf{w}) + \lambda \mathbf{w}) = (1 - \eta \lambda) \mathbf{w} - \eta \nabla \text{Loss}(\mathbf{w})
$$

### Geometric meaning: every step multiplies the weights by a number less than 1

Assume $(\eta = 0.1, \lambda = 0.01)$, then the decay coefficient = $(1 - 0.1 \times 0.01 = 0.999)$

After each update:

$$
\mathbf{w}_{\text{new}} = 0.999 \times \mathbf{w}_{\text{old}} - \eta \nabla \text{Loss}
$$

**After 100 updates**:

$$
\mathbf{w}_{\text{after 100 steps}} = 0.999^{100} \times \mathbf{w}_{\text{initial}} + \text{gradient contribution}
\approx 0.905 \times \mathbf{w}_{\text{initial}} + \text{gradient contribution}
$$

**Intuition**:

- Weights are **smoothly, proportionally shrunk**
- **All weights get smaller but never zeroed** (multiplying by a number < 1 each time never reaches 0)
- Like being **gently pulled back to the origin by a rubber band**

---

## ⚡ **2. L1 Weight Decay: Harsh "Subtractive Truncation"**

### Deriving the update formula

For L1 regularization, the loss function is:

$$
\text{TotalLoss} = \text{Loss}(\mathbf{w}) + \lambda \|\mathbf{w}\|_1
$$

Gradient (where $(w_i \neq 0)$):

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial w_i} \text{TotalLoss} = \frac{\partial \text{Loss}}{\partial w_i} + \lambda \cdot \text{sign}(w_i)
$$

**Weight update** (when $(w_i > 0)$):

$$
w_i \leftarrow w_i - \eta \frac{\partial \text{Loss}}{\partial w_i} - \eta \lambda
$$

**When $(w_i < 0)$**:

$$
w_i \leftarrow w_i - \eta \frac{\partial \text{Loss}}{\partial w_i} + \eta \lambda
$$

**When $(w_i = 0)$**:

- The gradient is undefined (the absolute value function is not differentiable at 0)
- In practice, when \(|w_i|\) is very small, just set it to 0

### Geometric meaning: every step subtracts a fixed value

Assume $(\eta = 0.1, \lambda = 0.01)$, then each update:

$$
w_i \leftarrow w_i - \eta \frac{\partial \text{Loss}}{\partial w_i} - 0.001 \quad (\text{if } w_i > 0)
$$

**Key insight**:

- **No matter how big $w_i$ is, the penalty is the fixed $(\eta \lambda)$**
- When the original gradient $(\frac{\partial \text{Loss}}{\partial w_i})$ is small, $w_i$ gets **pushed linearly back to 0**
- Once $w_i$ crosses 0, the sign flips and the penalty direction flips too

**Intuition**:

- Weights are **harshly pushed back to 0 in fixed steps**
- **When a weight is small enough, it gets directly "truncated" to 0**
- Like being **pulled to the origin by a magnet**

---

## 📈 **3. Comparative Analysis: The Weight-Decay View**

| Dimension | L2 weight decay | L1 weight decay |
| -------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Decay method** | multiplicative decay (multiply by a coefficient < 1) | subtractive decay (subtract a fixed value) |
| **Decay strength** | proportional to weight size | independent of weight size (fixed) |
| **Small-weight behavior** | the closer to 0, the weaker the decay | no matter how small, the decay strength stays the same |
| **Result** | weights shrink smoothly, never to zero | weights pushed back linearly, can reach zero |
| **Sparsity** | ❌ no sparse solution | ✅ sparse solution |
| **Math form** | $w \leftarrow (1-\eta\lambda)w - \eta \nabla$ | $w \leftarrow w - \eta \nabla - \eta\lambda \cdot \text{sign}(w)$ |
| **Physical analogy** | rubber band (elastic decay) | magnet (fixed attraction) |

---

## 🎨 **4. Visualizing the Weight Decay Process**

### Assume a weight $(w = 0.5)$, original gradient $(\nabla = 0.01)$, $(\eta = 0.1)$, $(\lambda = 0.1)$

**L2 decay (coefficient = 0.99)**:

```
Step 0: w = 0.500
Step 1: w = 0.99*0.5 - 0.1*0.01 = 0.495 - 0.001 = 0.494
Step 2: w = 0.99*0.494 - 0.001 = 0.489 - 0.001 = 0.488
Step 3: w = 0.99*0.488 - 0.001 = 0.483 - 0.001 = 0.482
...
Step 100: w ≈ 0.37 (never reaches 0)
```

**L1 decay (fixed penalty = 0.01)**:

```
Step 0: w = 0.500
Step 1: w = 0.5 - 0.001 - 0.01 = 0.489
Step 2: w = 0.489 - 0.001 - 0.01 = 0.478
Step 3: w = 0.478 - 0.001 - 0.01 = 0.467
...
Step 45: w = 0.005
Step 46: w = max(0, 0.005 - 0.001 - 0.01) = 0 (truncated to 0)
```

## ⚖️ **5. Choosing in Practice**

### L2 weight decay is good for:

- **Deep neural networks** (preventing overfitting)
- Scenarios where **all features matter**
- Tasks that **need to keep all weights**

### L1 weight decay is good for:

- **Feature selection** (automatic sparsification)
- **High-dimensional data** (features far outnumber samples)
- **Model compression** (reducing the parameter count)

**Classic examples**:

- **L2**: large models like ResNet, BERT
- **L1**: gene expression analysis, feature selection in recommendation systems

## 💻 **6. PyTorch Implementation Comparison**

### L2 weight decay (automatic)

```python
# specify weight_decay in the optimizer (that's L2)
optimizer = torch.optim.SGD(
    model.parameters(),
    lr=0.01,
    weight_decay=0.001  # L2 coefficient λ
)

# equivalent to:
# w ← (1 - lr*λ) * w - lr * grad
```

### L1 weight decay (manual)

```python
l1_lambda = 0.001

# in the training loop
optimizer.zero_grad()
loss = compute_loss(model, data)

# manually add the L1 penalty
l1_norm = sum(torch.norm(p, 1) for p in model.parameters())
total_loss = loss + l1_lambda * l1_norm

total_loss.backward()
optimizer.step()

# equivalent to:
# w ← w - lr * (grad + λ * sign(w))
```

### Industry Pitfall Guide

L2 regularization `≠` weight decay (in Adam)

- In SGD: `L2 Regularization` and `Weight Decay` are mathematically completely equivalent.
- In Adam: `L2 Regularization` and `Weight Decay` are different concepts.
  - If you add the L2 regularizer to the Loss ($ \text{Loss} + \lambda \|\mathbf{w}\|^2 $), Adam will compute its gradient and divide it by the second-moment estimate $\sqrt{v_t}$. This means the larger the parameter, the more **Adam shrinks the regularization strength** — defeating the purpose of regularization.
  - The right approach: use AdamW (Adam with Weight Decay). It decouples weight decay from the gradient update (directly doing $w \leftarrow w - \eta \lambda(w)$), so it's not affected by the second moment. This restores L2's classic `multiplicative decay` form (i.e., $w \leftarrow (1 - \eta \lambda)w$)

Conclusion: when using Adam, pick AdamW without thinking.

## 🎯 **7. One-Sentence Summary**

> **L2 weight decay**: every update multiplies the weights by a number less than 1 (like 0.999), smoothly and proportionally shrinking all weights — making them smaller but never 0.
> **L1 weight decay**: every update subtracts a fixed value from the weights (like 0.001), harshly and linearly pushing them back to 0, causing some weights to be truncated to 0.

That's why:

- **L2 is like a rubber band**: elastic decay, the weights are never "snapped off"
- **L1 is like scissors**: fixed trimming, small weights get directly "cut off" (sparsified)

---

### The Ultimate Memory Mnemonic

> **L2 multiplies by a coefficient, L1 subtracts a constant**
> **L2 keeps everyone, L1 chops the small**

## Explaining L1 & L2 Regularization from the Bayesian Angle

Looking at regularization from the Bayesian angle is like giving model parameters a `vaccine` — injecting some `prior belief` before training so the model doesn't easily get "sick" (overfit). L1 and L2 are two different `vaccine ingredients`, corresponding to different `health beliefs`.

---

## 🎯 **The Core Idea of the Bayesian View**

### Reviewing Bayes' formula

$$
P(\mathbf{w}|\text{Data}) = \frac{P(\text{Data}|\mathbf{w}) \cdot P(\mathbf{w})}{P(\text{Data})}
$$

- **$P(\mathbf{w}|\text{Data})$**: the posterior distribution (what the parameters should look like after seeing the data)
- **$P(\text{Data}|\mathbf{w})$**: the likelihood (how well the parameters explain the data)
- **$P(\mathbf{w})$**: the prior distribution (what we think the parameters should look like before training)
- **$P(\text{Data})$**: the evidence (a constant, ignorable)

**The essence of regularization**: the **prior distribution $P(\mathbf{w})$** constrains the "initial belief" about the parameters.

---

## 📐 **L2 Regularization = Gaussian Prior (Normal Distribution)**

### Mathematical form

Assume each weight $w_i$ follows a Gaussian distribution with mean 0 and variance $\sigma^2$:

$$
P(w_i) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma} \exp\left(-\frac{w_i^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)
$$

For all weights $\mathbf{w}$:

$$
P(\mathbf{w}) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} P(w_i) \propto \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i^2\right)
$$

Bayesian Maximum a Posteriori (MAP) estimation:

$$
\mathbf{w}_{\text{MAP}} = \arg\max_{\mathbf{w}} \left[ \ln P(\text{Data}|\mathbf{w}) + \ln P(\mathbf{w}) \right]
$$

Substituting the Gaussian prior:

$$
\mathbf{w}_{\text{MAP}} = \arg\max_{\mathbf{w}} \left[ \underbrace{\ln P(\text{Data}|\mathbf{w})}_{\text{negative loss}} - \underbrace{\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\|\mathbf{w}\|_2^2}_{\text{L2 regularization term}} \right]
$$

Equivalent to minimizing:

$$
\text{Loss}(\mathbf{w}) + \lambda \|\mathbf{w}\|_2^2
$$

where $\lambda = \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}$

---

### Intuition for the Gaussian prior

**Probability density plot**:

```
probability density
   ↑
   |      ○○○
   |    ○     ○
   |   ○       ○
   |  ○         ○
   | ○           ○
   +○-------------○→ w
  -3  -2  -1   0   1   2   3
```

**Reading the belief**:

- **Belief**: weights should be concentrated near 0
- **Shape**: a smooth bell curve
- **Strength**: the bigger the weight, the smaller the probability (the bigger the `penalty`)
- **Feature**: **gently** believes weights shouldn't be too big, but allows some fluctuation

**Life analogy**:

- Like a "lenient homeroom teacher": believes students should follow rules (near 0), but occasional mischief is acceptable
- Like a "rubber band": pull proportional to distance; the further from center, the stronger the pull

## 📈 **L1 Regularization = Laplace Prior (Laplace Distribution)**

### Mathematical form

Assume each weight $w_i$ follows a Laplace distribution with mean 0 and scale $b$:

$$
P(w_i) = \frac{1}{2b} \exp\left(-\frac{|w_i|}{b}\right)
$$

For all weights $\mathbf{w}$:

$$
P(\mathbf{w}) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} P(w_i) \propto \exp\left(-\frac{1}{b}\sum_{i=1}^{n} |w_i|\right)
$$

**Bayesian MAP estimation**:

$$
\mathbf{w}_{\text{MAP}} = \arg\max_{\mathbf{w}} \left[ \ln P(\text{Data}|\mathbf{w}) - \frac{1}{b}\|\mathbf{w}\|_1 \right]
$$

**Equivalent to minimizing**:

$$
\text{Loss}(\mathbf{w}) + \lambda \|\mathbf{w}\|_1
$$

where $\lambda = \frac{1}{b}$

---

### Intuition for the Laplace prior

**Probability density plot**:

```
probability density
   ↑
   |    ◇
   |   / \
   |  /   \
   | /     \
   |/       \
   +○--------○→ w
  -3  -2  -1   0   1   2   3
```

**Reading the belief**:

- **Belief**: the weight is **very likely exactly 0**, occasionally deviating
- **Shape**: a **sharp spike** at 0
- **Strength**: the bigger the weight, the **exponentially decaying** probability
- **Feature**: **strongly** believes weights should be 0, with only a few allowed to be nonzero

**Life analogy**:

- Like a "strict homeroom teacher": believes students **should** follow the rules (=0), with only very few special exceptions
- Like a "magnet": a fixed attraction at 0; once you get near, you're pulled in (to 0)

## 🔍 **Key Difference: The Shape of the Prior Determines the Effect of Regularization**

### Probability density comparison

```
probability density
   ↑
   |      ○○○         ◇
   |    ○     ○      / \
   |   ○       ○    /   \
   |  ○         ○  /     \
   | ○           ○/       \
---+○-------------○○--------○→ w
   |  Gaussian prior   Laplace prior
```

| Feature | Gaussian prior (L2) | Laplace prior (L1) |
| ------------------------- | ------------- | ------------------------ |
| **Probability density at 0** | moderate | **extreme (spike)** |
| **Tail probability** | high (thick tail) | low (thin tail) |
| **Belief about large weights** | gentle penalty | strong penalty |
| **Solution produced** | dense | **sparse** |

## 🧮 **Bayesian Mathematical Derivation (Simplified)**

### Why Does L1 Produce Sparse Solutions?

**Posterior probability**:

$$
P(\mathbf{w}|\text{Data}) \propto P(\text{Data}|\mathbf{w}) \cdot P(\mathbf{w})
$$

**L2 case**:

$$
P(\mathbf{w}) \propto \exp\left(-\lambda \sum w_i^2\right)
$$

- When $w_i = 0$, $P(w_i=0) = \text{finite value}$
- When $w_i \neq 0$, $P(w_i) \propto \exp(-\lambda w_i^2)$ decays slowly
- **Conclusion**: the model doesn't "strongly believe" the weights must be 0

**L1 case**:

$$
P(\mathbf{w}) \propto \exp\left(-\lambda \sum |w_i|\right)
$$

- When $w_i = 0$, $P(w_i=0) = \text{extreme value (spike)}$
- When $w_i \neq 0$, $P(w_i) \propto \exp(-\lambda |w_i|)$ decays exponentially
- **Conclusion**: the model "strongly believes" weights should be 0 and only deviates when the data evidence is strong enough

## 💡 **Core Insights of the Bayesian Explanation**

### 1. Regularization = Injecting Prior Knowledge

- **L2**: we **a priori believe** the weights should be small (but not necessarily 0)
- **L1**: we **a priori believe** the weights are very likely 0 (only a few matter)

### 2. MAP = Maximum Likelihood + Prior Penalty

$$
\underbrace{\text{minimizing loss}}_{\text{maximum likelihood}} + \underbrace{\lambda \|\mathbf{w}\|}_{\text{prior penalty}}
$$

### 3. The Bayesian Meaning of the Lagrange Multiplier $\lambda$

$$
\lambda = \frac{1}{\text{variance of the prior distribution}}
$$

- Bigger $\lambda$ → smaller prior variance → stronger prior → weights squashed tighter
- Smaller $\lambda$ → larger prior variance → weaker prior → close to unconstrained

### 4. What Does $\lambda$ Really Represent?

In Bayes' formula, we derived $\lambda \propto \frac{1}{\sigma^2}$ (the reciprocal of the prior distribution's variance)

- Bigger $\lambda$, $\to {\sigma}^2$ smaller, $\to$ the Gaussian/Laplace distribution becomes very **tall and skinny**, $\to$ very confident the weights should be near 0 (an extremely strong prior) $\to$ the model prefers simpler solutions.
- Smaller $\lambda$, $\to {\sigma}^2$ larger, $\to$ the distribution is very flat, $\to$ no strong opinion about weight size, let the data speak (weak prior) $\to$ the model prefers more complex solutions.

## 🎯 **Practical Application Advice (Bayesian View)**

### When to use L2 (Gaussian prior)?

- **When you think most features are useful**, just with different importance
- Features are **strongly correlated**
- Need **numerical stability** (smooth and differentiable)
- **The default choice** (most deep learning tasks)

### When to use L1 (Laplace prior)?

- **When you think only a few features are useful** and most are noise
- **Feature dimension far larger than sample count** (high-dimensional sparse problems)
- Need **feature selection** or **model compression**
- High interpretability requirements (knowing which features matter)

### Mixed use (Elastic Net)

$$
\text{Loss} + \lambda_1 ||\mathbf{w}||_1 + \lambda_2 ||\mathbf{w}||_2^2
$$

- **Prior = Laplace + Gaussian**
- Combines L1's sparsity with L2's stability

> **L1 regularization = Laplace prior = "strongly believing the weights should be 0"**
