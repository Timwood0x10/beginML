# Neural Geometry: Dimensions & Parameters

**Abstract**: In deep learning, **Dimensions** define the shape of information and the topology of its flow, while **Parameters** are the entities that carry knowledge within these structures. Understanding the mathematical relationship between the two is a prerequisite for designing model capacity, estimating memory usage, and deriving Scaling Laws. This chapter starts from **affine transformations**, then dives into CNN's **computational density (FLOPs)**, the Transformer's **quadratic scaling law**, and the **continuous-time control equations (SSM)** behind Mamba.

---

## 1. The Physical Meaning of Dimensions and the Algebraic Essence of Parameters

### 1.1 Skeleton and Flesh
If we compare a neural network to a living organism:
*   **Dimensions** are the **skeleton**: they define how tensors flow, fold, and unfold in space ($(B, C, H, W)$ or $(B, T, D)$).
*   **Parameters** are the **flesh**: they are the real-valued weights the model learns via gradient descent (Weights $\mathbf{W}$ and Biases $\mathbf{b}$).

### 1.2 Degrees of Freedom & Capacity
Mathematically, the total number of parameters $N$ represents the model's **degrees of freedom**.
According to **VC dimension theory**, a model's capacity (memory power) usually correlates positively with $N$.
*   **Over-parameterization**: a counterintuitive phenomenon of modern deep learning is that $N$ is often far larger than the number of training samples. This makes the loss surface have countless global minima, turning optimization into a "walk" on the parameter manifold.

---

## 2. Fully Connected Layers: Affine Transformations in High-Dimensional Space

This is the most basic atomic operation of neural networks, corresponding to `nn.Linear` in PyTorch.

### 2.1 Mathematical Form
Let the input vector be $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{in}}$ and the output vector $\mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{out}}$:
$$ \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{W}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b} $$
where $\mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{out} \times d_{in}}$, $\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{out}}$.

### 2.2 Parameter Counting
$$ N_{linear} = \underbrace{(d_{in} \times d_{out})}_{\text{Weights}} + \underbrace{d_{out}}_{\text{Biases}} $$

*   **Geometric view**: this is a **full mapping** between two high-dimensional vector spaces. Every dimension of the input space interacts with every dimension of the output space (dense connection).
*   **Complexity**: $O(d_{in} \cdot d_{out})$. If $d_{in} \approx d_{out} = D$, the parameter count grows **quadratically** as $O(D^2)$.


### 2.3 Low-Rank Geometry: LoRA's Parameter Compression
In large-model fine-tuning, we find the **update** $\Delta \mathbf{W}$ of the weights doesn't need to be full-rank.
**LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation)** assumes updates happen in a low-dimensional subspace:
$$ \mathbf{W}_{new} = \mathbf{W}_{old} + \Delta \mathbf{W} = \mathbf{W}_{old} + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{A} $$
where $\mathbf{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times r}, \mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times D}$, and $r \ll D$ (e.g., $r=8$).

*   **Compression ratio**:
    $$ \frac{\text{LoRA Params}}{\text{Full Params}} = \frac{2 \times r \times D}{D^2} = \frac{2r}{D} $$
    When $D=4096, r=8$, the compression ratio is $16/4096 \approx 0.4\%$. This is the ultimate application of **geometric dimension manipulation**.

## 3. Convolutional Layers: Locality and Computational Density (CNN)

CNNs exploit the geometric priors of image data: **Locality** and **Translation Invariance**.

### 3.1 Weight Sharing
Unlike fully connected layers which assign independent weights to each pixel, convolutional layers share a single kernel $\mathcal{K}$ across the whole spatial extent $(H, W)$.

### 3.2 Parameters vs Computation (FLOPs)
Let the input tensor be $\mathcal{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{C_{in} \times H \times W}$, the kernel $K \times K$, and the output channels $C_{out}$.

1.  **Parameter count (static memory)**: **independent** of image size $H, W$.
    $$ N_{conv} = C_{in} \times K \times K \times C_{out} + C_{out} $$
    
2.  **Computation (FLOPs - dynamic compute)**: **proportional** to image size $H, W$.
    $$ \text{FLOPs} \approx 2 \times H \times W \times (C_{in} \times K^2 \times C_{out}) $$
    *(the factor 2 represents one multiply plus one add)*

### 3.3 Key Insights
*   **Spatial independence**: the parameter count is decoupled from spatial size, letting CNNs handle arbitrary resolutions.
*   **Compute black hole**: although parameters are modest, high-resolution images ($4K$) make FLOPs explode. That's why MobileNet uses **depthwise separable convolutions** (decomposing $C_{in} \times C_{out}$ into $C_{in} + C_{out}$).

---

## 4. Recurrent Layers: Recursive Unrolling in Time (RNN)

RNNs process sequence data; their core assumption is **Temporal Invariance** — using the same set of weights at every time step.

### 4.1 State Transition Equation
$$ \mathbf{h}_t = \sigma(\mathbf{W}_{ih}\mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{W}_{hh}\mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{b}) $$

### 4.2 Parameter Composition (Standard RNN)
1.  **Input-hidden** $\mathbf{W}_{ih}$: $D_{h} \times D_{in}$
2.  **Hidden-hidden** $\mathbf{W}_{hh}$: $D_{h} \times D_{h}$
3.  **Total parameters**: $N_{rnn} \approx (D_{in} + D_{h}) \times D_{h}$

### 4.3 LSTM's Geometric Complexity
LSTM introduces 4 gating mechanisms, equivalent to training 4 RNNs at once:
$$ N_{lstm} = 4 \times N_{rnn} \approx 4 \times (D_{in} + D_{h}) \times D_{h} $$

*   **Geometric view**: the $D_{h} \times D_{h}$ term shows that RNN memory capacity grows **quadratically** with the hidden dimension.

---

## 5. Transformer: Subspace Projection and the Attention Manifold

The Transformer completely abandons inductive biases in favor of **Set Interaction**.

### 5.1 Embedding & Projection
The Transformer's core dimension is $d_{model}$.
*   **Embedding**: a lookup operation, with parameters $V \times d_{model}$ ($V$ is the vocab size, usually huge).
*   **Q/K/V projections**: map inputs into different subspaces. Although there are multiple heads (Multi-Head), the total parameter count stays the same.
    $$ 
    \begin{aligned} 
    N_{attn} &\approx 4 \times d_{model}^2 \quad (W_Q, W_K, W_V, W_O) 
    \end{aligned} 
    $$

### 5.2 The FFN's Dimension Expansion
The FFN usually has a "wide" intermediate layer $d_{ff} = 4d_{model}$.
$$ 
\begin{aligned}
N_{ffn} &= (d_{model} \times d_{ff}) + (d_{ff} \times d_{model}) = 8 d_{model}^2 
\end{aligned}
$$

### 5.3 Block Parameters
A standard Transformer Block has roughly:
$$ N_{block} \approx 12 d_{model}^2 $$
This explains the **LLM Scaling Law**: parameters scale with the square of the model width.

---

## 6. Mamba (SSM): A Control-Theoretic Geometric Realization

Mamba (S4) tries to balance the RNN's recursive efficiency with the Transformer's parallelism.

### 6.1 Core Equations: A Continuous-Time System (ODE)
Mamba's mathematical core is no longer matrix multiplication but the differential equations of a **State Space Model**:
$$ \frac{d\mathbf{x}(t)}{dt} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}(t) + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}(t) $$
$$ \mathbf{y}(t) = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{x}(t) $$
*   $\mathbf{u}(t)$: input signal (1D sequence).
*   $\mathbf{x}(t)$: latent state (dimension $N$).
*   $\mathbf{y}(t)$: output signal.

### 6.2 Discretization and Parameter Flow
To run on a computer, the ODE must be discretized into a recursive form via **Zero-Order Hold (ZOH)**.
Parameters mostly live in **linear projections**, used to dynamically generate $\Delta, \mathbf{B}, \mathbf{C}$ from the input:
1.  **Input Projections**: $d_{model} \to d_{inner}$.
2.  **SSM Parameters**: $\mathbf{B}, \mathbf{C}, \Delta$ are **Input-Dependent**.
3.  **Output Projection**: $d_{inner} \to d_{model}$.

### 6.3 Parameter Efficiency
$$ N_{mamba} \propto E \cdot d_{model}^2 $$
Although the formula looks similar to the Transformer (both scale with $d_{model}^2$), Mamba **removes Attention's $O(T^2)$ computational complexity**, achieving linear scaling at inference time.

## 7. Code Practice


### 7.1 Macro View: Parameter Growth Curves

To intuitively understand why Scaling Laws are so fascinating, we plot parameter growth curves for different architectures as dimension $D$ varies.

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def plot_scaling_laws():
    """
    Visualize how parameter counts grow with Hidden Size (d_model) for different architectures
    """
    # define the range of d_model (from 128 to 4096)
    d_model = np.linspace(128, 4096, 100)
    
    # 1. Linear / RNN (single layer): ~ d^2
    # assume d_in = d_out = d
    params_linear = d_model**2 + d_model
    
    # 2. Transformer Block: ~ 12 * d^2
    # (4 * d^2 from Attn + 8 * d^2 from FFN)
    params_transformer = 12 * d_model**2
    
    # 3. Mamba Block: ~ E * d^2 (assume E=2, with some overhead ≈ 6 * d^2)
    # this is a rough estimate, depends on implementation details
    params_mamba = 6 * d_model**2
    
    # 4. CNN (ResNet Block): ~ C * K^2 * C
    # assume Kernel=3, C = d_model
    params_cnn = 9 * d_model**2

    plt.figure(figsize=(10, 6))
    
    plt.plot(d_model, params_linear, label='Single Linear / RNN Layer ($N \propto D^2$)', linestyle='--')
    plt.plot(d_model, params_cnn, label='Conv Block 3x3 ($N \propto 9 D^2$)')
    plt.plot(d_model, params_mamba, label='Mamba Block ($N \propto 6 D^2$)')
    plt.plot(d_model, params_transformer, label='Transformer Block ($N \propto 12 D^2$)', linewidth=3)
    
    plt.title("Scaling Laws: Parameter Growth by Model Dimension", fontsize=14)
    plt.xlabel("Model Dimension ($d_{model}$)", fontsize=12)
    plt.ylabel("Number of Parameters (Millions)", fontsize=12)
    plt.legend()
    plt.grid(True, alpha=0.3)
    
    # mark commonly used LLM dimensions
    # 768 (Bert Base), 4096 (Llama 7B)
    plt.axvline(768, color='gray', linestyle=':', alpha=0.5)
    plt.text(800, 0.2e8, 'Bert Base (768)', rotation=90)
    
    plt.axvline(4096, color='gray', linestyle=':', alpha=0.5)
    plt.text(4150, 0.2e8, 'Llama (4096)', rotation=90)
    
    # format Y axis in millions (M)
    current_values = plt.gca().get_yticks()
    plt.gca().set_yticklabels(['{:.0f}M'.format(x/1e6) for x in current_values])
    
    plt.tight_layout()
    plt.show()

if __name__ == "__main__":
    plot_scaling_laws()

```

![scaling low](../images/scaling_laws.png)

Reading the results:

* Quadratic dominance: the parameter count of all modern architectures scales quadratically with $d_{model}$ ($N \propto D^2$). This means every time we double the model width, parameters (and compute needs) grow 4x.

* The Transformer's cost: at the same $d_{model}$, one Transformer Block has about 1.5-2x the parameters of a same-width CNN or Mamba. That's because it has an extremely dense FFN layer ($4 \times 4$ wide).

* Dimension planning: when designing a model, if you only have 12G of VRAM, you must choose $d_{model}$ very carefully, because its impact on memory is quadratic.


### 7.2 Micro View: A Geometry Analyzer for Deep Networks
```python

import torch
import torch.nn as nn

class GeometryAnalyzer:
    def __init__(self, model):
        self.model = model

    def analyze(self, input_size):
        print(f"{'Layer':<20} | {'Input Shape':<20} | {'Output Shape':<20} | {'Params':<10}")
        print("-" * 80)
        
        hooks = [] # moved here to ensure correct scope

        # use the Hook mechanism to capture intermediate layer shapes
        def register_hook(module):
            def hook(module, input, output):
                class_name = str(module.__class__).split(".")[-1].split("'")[0]
                
                # count this layer's parameters (only current layer, not recursive)
                params = sum(p.numel() for p in module.parameters(recurse=False))
                
                # --- 1. handle input shape (Input is always a tuple) ---
                try:
                    # input[0] is usually the main input tensor
                    in_shape = str(list(input[0].shape))
                except:
                    in_shape = "n/a"

                # --- 2. handle output shape (key fix point) ---
                # LSTM/GRU return a tuple: (output, (hn, cn))
                # we only care about the shape of the first element (output)
                try:
                    if isinstance(output, (tuple, list)):
                        out_shape = str(list(output[0].shape))
                    else:
                        out_shape = str(list(output.shape))
                except:
                    out_shape = "n/a"
                
                # record the info
                print(f"{class_name:<20} | {in_shape:<20} | {out_shape:<20} | {params:<10}")
            
            # filter out container layers, only hook leaf nodes
            if not isinstance(module, nn.Sequential) and \
               not isinstance(module, nn.ModuleList) and \
               not (module == self.model):
                hooks.append(module.register_forward_hook(hook))
        
        self.model.apply(register_hook)
        
        # run one forward pass
        # make sure the input is on the right device (if the model is on GPU, change this too; defaults to CPU here)
        dummy_input = torch.zeros(*input_size)
        try:
            self.model(dummy_input)
        except Exception as e:
            print(f"Forward pass failed: {e}")
        finally:
            # regardless of success or failure, remove Hooks to avoid polluting the model
            for h in hooks:
                h.remove()
            
        # count total parameters
        total_params = sum(p.numel() for p in self.model.parameters())
        print("-" * 80)
        print(f"Total Model Parameters: {total_params:,}")

# === define a hybrid architecture model for testing ===
class HybridModel(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__()
        # 1. convolutional layer (spatial feature extraction)
        self.conv = nn.Conv2d(3, 16, kernel_size=3, padding=1)
        # 2. flatten (note: Flatten itself has no parameters)
        self.flatten = nn.Flatten()
        # 3. recurrent layer (simulates sequence processing)
        # assume we flatten (B, 16, 32, 32) and reshape before input
        # input_size=1024 (32*32), hidden_size=64
        self.rnn = nn.LSTM(input_size=32*32, hidden_size=64, batch_first=True)
        # 4. fully connected layer (output)
        self.fc = nn.Linear(64, 10)

    def forward(self, x):
        # x: (B, 3, 32, 32)
        x = self.conv(x)      # -> (B, 16, 32, 32)
        x = self.flatten(x)   # -> (B, 16*32*32) = (B, 16384)
        
        # key step: reshape to (Batch, Seq_Len, Input_Dim)
        # we treat Channel (16) as the sequence length and Spatial (32*32) as the feature dimension
        # View/Reshape usually don't trigger Hooks because they aren't nn.Modules
        x = x.view(x.size(0), 16, -1) # -> (B, 16, 1024)
        
        out, _ = self.rnn(x)  # -> (B, 16, 64)
        x = out[:, -1, :]     # take the last time step -> (B, 64)
        x = self.fc(x)        # -> (B, 10)
        return x

# === run the analysis ===
if __name__ == "__main__":
    model = HybridModel()
    analyzer = GeometryAnalyzer(model)
    # input: Batch=1, Channels=3, H=32, W=32
    analyzer.analyze((1, 3, 32, 32))
```

Result analysis

```shell
Layer                | Input Shape          | Output Shape         | Params    
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Conv2d               | [1, 3, 32, 32]       | [1, 16, 32, 32]      | 448       
Flatten              | [1, 16, 32, 32]      | [1, 16384]           | 0         
LSTM                 | [1, 16, 1024]        | [1, 16, 64]          | 279040    
Linear               | [1, 64]              | [1, 10]              | 650       
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Total Model Parameters: 280,138
```

Running the above code, you'll see the clear dimension flow:
* Conv2d: the parameter count depends only on channels and kernel size, independent of the 32x32 image.
* View/Reshape: no parameters — it's a pure geometric transformation (changing the manifold's topology without changing content).
* LSTM: huge parameter count, because it involves interaction matrices between input_size (1024) and hidden_size (64).
* Linear: maps the 64-dim latent space to the 10-dim class space.

Why does LSTM have 279,040 parameters? Let's verify with the formula from section 4.3:

Formula:
$$
N_{lstm}=4\times((D_{in}+D_{h})\times D_{h}+D_{h})
$$
(including Bias)
Data:
$D_{in}=1024 (32 \times 32)$
$D_{h}=64$

$$

\begin{aligned}
N_{lstm} &= 4 \times ( (1024 + 64) \times 64 + 64 ) \
&= 4 \times ( 1088 \times 64 + 64 ) \
&= 4 \times ( 69632 + 64 ) \
&= 4 \times 69696 \
&= \mathbf{279,040}
\end{aligned}
$$
Conclusion: the theoretical formula matches the measured code perfectly. This proves our mathematical understanding of neural network geometry is accurate.


### 7.3 RNN Temporal Unrolling Visualization

```python
def plot_rnn_unroll(timesteps=5):
    """
    Visualize the geometric unrolling of an RNN along the time dimension
    """
    import networkx as nx
    
    G = nx.DiGraph()
    pos = {}
    
    for t in range(timesteps):
        # nodes: input x, hidden state h, output y
        G.add_node(f'x_{t}', layer='input')
        G.add_node(f'h_{t}', layer='hidden')
        G.add_node(f'y_{t}', layer='output')
        
        # geometric positions
        pos[f'x_{t}'] = (t, 0)
        pos[f'h_{t}'] = (t, 1)
        pos[f'y_{t}'] = (t, 2)
        
        # edges: input -> hidden
        G.add_edge(f'x_{t}', f'h_{t}', color='blue')
        # edges: hidden -> output
        G.add_edge(f'h_{t}', f'y_{t}', color='green')
        
        # edges: temporal recurrence h_{t-1} -> h_t
        if t > 0:
            G.add_edge(f'h_{t-1}', f'h_{t}', color='red', weight=2)

    plt.figure(figsize=(10, 4))
    colors = [G[u][v].get('color', 'black') for u,v in G.edges()]
    nx.draw(G, pos, with_labels=True, node_color='lightgray', edge_color=colors, 
            node_size=2000, font_size=10, arrowsize=20)
    
    plt.title("RNN Geometry: Temporal Unrolling (Time as a Dimension)")
    # add legend
    plt.plot([], [], color='blue', label='Input Weights $W_{ih}$')
    plt.plot([], [], color='red', label='Recurrent Weights $W_{hh}$')
    plt.plot([], [], color='green', label='Output Weights $W_{ho}$')
    plt.legend(loc='upper right')
    plt.ylim(-0.5, 2.5)
    plt.show()
```

## 8. Summary: The Art of Dimension Design

Designing a neural network is essentially trading off expressive power (Capacity) against computational cost (Cost):

* Fully connected: the most flexible, but the full $O(D^2)$ parameters cause poor generalization.
* Convolutional: exploits spatial symmetry with extreme weight sharing, efficiently handling grid data. $O(1)$ parameters, $O(HW)$ computation, ideal for grid data.
* Transformer: $O(D^2)$ parameters, $O(T^2)$ computation — expensive, but captures global associations. Using the attention mechanism, parameters grow quadratically with dimension while gaining a global receptive field.

* Mamba/RNN: exploits temporal recurrence, extremely memory-efficient at inference. Using the differential equation 
$ \frac{dx}{dt}=Ax+Bu $, it achieves $O(T)$ linear computational complexity — the new hope for ultra-long sequences.
