# Training Dynamics and Hyperparameter Theory: From **Alchemy** to **Chemistry**

⚠️ Core insight: hyperparameters aren't static configuration — they're dynamic control

In previous notes, we focused on **model structure (what the car looks like)**.
This note focuses on **the training process (how hard to press the accelerator, how to turn the steering wheel)**.

Many beginners think tuning is random trial and error (Grid Search), but experienced researchers know that hyperparameters are tightly mathematically coupled:

1. Initialization: determines whether the signal in a deep network `dies` (vanishes) or `explodes` (diverges).
2. Normalization: changes the geometric curvature of the loss function, making optimization smoother.
3. Signal-to-noise ratio (SDE): the learning rate ($\eta$) and Batch Size ($B$) together determine the `noise temperature` during training, directly affecting generalization.

---

## 1. The Physics of Initialization: Signal Propagation Theory

Why can't we initialize all weights to 0? Why can't we use a standard Gaussian $N(0,1)$?
This involves a core problem: Variance Preservation.

### 1.1 The Signal's `Life and Death`

Consider a simple linear layer $y=Wx$. Assume $x$ and $W$ are independent with mean 0.
By variance properties:

$$
\text{Var}\left(y\right) = \text{Var}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\text{in}}} w_i x_i\right) \approx n_{\text{in}} \cdot \text{Var}(w_i) \cdot \text{Var}(x_i)
$$

- If $n_{in} \cdot \text{Var}(w_i) > 1$, the signal is amplified at every layer, eventually `exploding` and diverging (deep network numerical explosion (NaN)).

- If $n_{in} \cdot \text{Var}(w_i) < 1$, the signal shrinks at every layer, eventually `dying` and vanishing (deep network degenerates into a linear model).

- If $n_{in} \cdot \text{Var}(w_i) = 1$, the signal stays constant at every layer and propagates stably.

Goal: we want the signal alive at every layer, i.e., $Var(y)=Var(x)$.

$$

\implies \text{Var}(w) = \frac{1}{n_{\text{in}}}

$$

This is the origin of Xavier Initialization (Glorot Init), suitable for Tanh/Sigmoid activations.

### 1.2 Kaiming (He) Initialization: The ReLU Fix

The ReLU activation $f(x)=max(0,x)$ zeroes out half the neurons. That means it kills half the variance.
To compensate for the lost half of the energy, the weight variance must double:

$$

\frac{1}{2} n_{\text{in}} \text{Var}(w) = 1 \implies \text{Var}(w) = \frac{2}{n_{\text{in}}}

$$

Conclusion: with ReLU, weights must be initialized to $\mathcal{N}(0, \sqrt{\frac{2}{n_{\text{in}}}})$. Otherwise deep networks simply can't be trained.

## 2. The Geometric Essence of Normalization: Smoothing the Landscape


Batch Normalization (BN) and Layer Normalization (LN) are standard equipment in modern deep learning.
The early explanation was `solving internal covariate shift`, but MIT research shows this isn't the root cause.

### 2.1 The Real Mathematical Role: A Lipschitz Constraint

Normalization bounds the rate at which the loss function $L$'s gradient changes (i.e., the Lipschitz constant).

$$
\left\|\nabla L(x) - \nabla L(y)\right\| \le K\left\|x - y\right\|
$$

BN/LN makes $K$ smaller.

- Geometric intuition: if you're mountain climbing, normalization flattens the `cliffs` (places where gradients change violently) into gentle `slopes`.
- Engineering consequence: you can use a larger learning rate without divergence.

### 2.2 Pre-Norm vs Post-Norm (The Transformer's Evolution)

- Post-Norm (original Transformer): `x = Norm(x + Sublayer(x))`. Gradients must pass through the Norm layer during backpropagation, so gradients easily vanish in deep stacks. Needs strong Warmup to train.

- Pre-Norm (LLaMA / GPT-3): `x = x + Sublayer(Norm(x))`. The main road is an Identity Path; gradients flow directly to the first layer. Extremely stable training; the standard for current large models.

## 3. The Stochastic Process of Training: SGD Is an SDE

The hyperparameters Learning Rate ($\eta$) and Batch Size ($B$) aren't independent — they're twins.

### 3.1 The SDE View
SGD's update formula is essentially the discretization of an SDE. Mandt et al. (2017) proved SGD approximates:

$$
d\theta_t = -\nabla L(\theta_t)dt + \sqrt{\frac{\eta}{B} C(\theta_t)} dW_t
$$

where $W_t$ is Brownian motion and $C(\theta_t)$ is a covariance matrix. $\frac {\eta}{B}$ is the diffusion coefficient (noise intensity).


### 3.2 Noise Temperature and Generalization

Treat $\frac {\eta}{B}$ as the temperature $T$ in thermodynamics. The larger $\frac {\eta}{B}$, the more noise, the more unstable the model, the worse the generalization.

- Large Batch (small noise/low temperature): the model quickly falls into the nearest minimum. If this pit is narrow (Sharp Minima), the Loss explodes when the test set shifts slightly. Poor generalization.

- Small Batch (large noise/high temperature): the model bounces around in the pit; only wide, flat minima (Flat Minima) can keep it. Good generalization.

### 3.3 The Linear Scaling Rule

If you scale the Batch Size up by $k$ (to speed up training), you must also scale the learning rate up by $k$ to keep the `noise temperature` constant.

$$
\eta_{new} = k\cdot \eta_{old}  \\ when \space \space  B_{new} = k \cdot B_{old}
$$

This is the single most important iron law in distributed training (e.g., training ImageNet/LLMs).

## 4. The Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK)
When we push the network width toward infinity, something magical happens: the training dynamics linearize.
### 4.1 Lazy Training
In the infinite-width limit, the weights $w$ barely move from initialization, yet the Loss still drops to 0.
At this point, the neural network is equivalent to a Kernel Regression whose kernel is determined by the network structure, called the NTK.

$$

f(x,t) \approx f(x,0) + \nabla f(x,0)^T (w_t - w_0)

$$

This explains why over-parameterized large models easily fit training data — they're essentially doing linear interpolation in high-dimensional space.

## 5. Engineering Practice: Visualizing Signal Propagation

This code intuitively shows how a wrong initialization kills a 50-layer deep neural network.

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import numpy as np

# === 1. define a deep network forward-propagation test ===
def test_initialization(activation_fn, init_method, layer_num=50):
    # simulate input data: mean 0, variance 1
    x = torch.randn(512, 512) 
    
    activations = []
    
    for i in range(layer_num):
        layer = nn.Linear(512, 512)
        
        # apply different initialization methods
        if init_method == 'std_normal':
            # standard normal: std=1 (wrong method)
            nn.init.normal_(layer.weight, mean=0, std=1.0) 
        elif init_method == 'kaiming':
            # Kaiming He Init: std = sqrt(2/n)
            nn.init.kaiming_normal_(layer.weight, nonlinearity='relu')
            
        # forward propagation
        x = layer(x)
        x = activation_fn(x)
        
        # record the mean and std of each layer
        activations.append(x.std().item())
        
        # stop early if values explode or vanish
        if torch.isnan(x).any() or x.std().item() < 1e-6:
            break
            
    return activations

# === 2. run the experiments ===
# Experiment A: ReLU + standard Gaussian init (expected: vanishing/exploding gradients)
std_acts = test_initialization(nn.ReLU(), 'std_normal')

# Experiment B: ReLU + Kaiming init (expected: stable variance)
kaiming_acts = test_initialization(nn.ReLU(), 'kaiming')

# === 3. visualize ===
plt.figure(figsize=(10, 5))
plt.plot(std_acts, label='Standard Normal (std=1)', marker='x', color='red', linestyle='--')
plt.plot(kaiming_acts, label='Kaiming Init', color='green', linewidth=2)

plt.title("Signal Propagation in Deep Networks (50 Layers)", fontsize=14)
plt.xlabel("Layer Depth")
plt.ylabel("Activation Standard Deviation")
plt.yscale('log') # log scale makes vanishing/exploding clearer
plt.axhline(y=1.0, color='gray', linestyle=':', alpha=0.5)
plt.legend()
plt.grid(True, alpha=0.3)
plt.show()
```


### Reading the Experimental Results

![](../images/signal_propagation.png)


- Red line (Standard Normal): around layers 5-10, the variance either spikes to NaN (explosion) or drops to 0 (vanishing), depending on the specific activation and dimensions. Under ReLU it usually appears as uncontrollable violent oscillation.

- Green line (Kaiming Init): the variance stays near 1.0 across the 50-layer network. This means the signal successfully `survives` to the last layer, and gradients successfully backpropagate.

## 6. The Ultimate Summary: The Chemical Equation of Tuning
|Symptom|Diagnosis|Prescription|
|---|---|---|
|Loss doesn't drop|Signal vanishes in deep layers / initial gradients too small|Check Kaiming Init (ReLU) or Xavier Init (Tanh)|
|Loss oscillates/diverges|Loss terrain too steep (large Lipschitz constant)|Add BatchNorm / LayerNorm; reduce LR|
|Trains well but generalizes poorly|Fell into a sharp minimum (Sharp Minima)|Increase Noise Temperature ($\frac {\eta}{B}$): raise LR or reduce Batch Size|
|Deep Transformer hard to train|Gradient flow blocked in backprop|Switch to Pre-Norm structure|
|Large Batch training fails|Too little noise; SGD became GD|Use the Linear Scaling Rule to increase LR linearly|

One-sentence summary:

Tuning isn't mysticism — it's the dynamics of controlling signal variance in high-dimensional space (initialization/normalization) and controlling the temperature of optimization (learning rate/Batch Size).
