# Probability & Information Theory: Quantifying Uncertainty

**Abstract**: If calculus is the engine of deep learning and linear algebra is the container of data, then probability theory is AI's **worldview**. Machine learning essentially builds a probability distribution $P_{\theta}(y|x)$ to fit the real world. This chapter doesn't talk about coin flips; instead it goes deep into **the core metrics of LLMs (entropy and perplexity)**, rigorously derives the **non-negativity of KL divergence** via **Jensen's inequality**, analyzes the role of **JSD** in GANs, and explores the counterintuitive **high-dimensional probability theory** — revealing why cosine similarity beats Euclidean distance in high-dimensional vector retrieval (RAG).

---

## 1. Frequentist vs Bayesian

### 1.1 Two Worldviews
*   **Frequentist**: the parameter $\theta$ is a fixed objective truth — we just don't know it. We approach it through lots of data (Maximum Likelihood Estimation, MLE).
    *   *Application*: most neural network training (finding one optimal set of weights).
*   **Bayesian**: the parameter $\theta$ itself is a random variable following some distribution. We update our belief about $\theta$ with data (the posterior distribution).
    *   *Application*: LoRA (initialization prior), diffusion models (Markov chains), uncertainty quantification.

### 1.2 Bayes' Formula from an AI Perspective
$$ P(\theta | \text{Data}) = \frac{P(\text{Data} | \theta) \cdot P(\theta)}{P(\text{Data})} $$
*   **Posterior**: the parameter distribution after training.
*   **Likelihood**: the probability of producing this data under the current parameters (the source of Loss).
*   **Prior**: our preset assumption about the parameters (L2 regularization is essentially assuming the weights follow a Gaussian prior).

## 2. Information Theory: The Measuring Stick of LLMs

Training a large language model (LLM) is essentially compressing all the text information on the internet.

### 2.1 Surprisal & Entropy
The amount of information equals the "degree of surprise".
$$ I(x) = -\log P(x) $$
**Entropy** is the expected information, measuring a distribution's uncertainty:
$$ H(P) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P} [-\log P(x)] $$
*   **Physical meaning**: the average minimum number of bits needed to encode this distribution.

### 2.2 Cross-Entropy & Perplexity
When training an LLM, the true distribution $P$ is unknown (human language); the model's distribution is $Q$.
**Cross-entropy**: the average number of bits needed to encode the true distribution $P$ using the model $Q$.
$$ H(P, Q) = -\sum P(x) \log Q(x) $$
This is exactly the LLM's Loss function.

**Perplexity (PPL)**:
$$ \text{PPL} = 2^{H(P, Q)} $$
*   Intuition: if PPL=10, the model, when predicting the next Token, feels like it's rolling dice among 10 equally likely options.

### 2.3 KL Divergence and the Jensen's Inequality Proof
KL divergence (Relative Entropy) measures the difference between two distributions:
$$ D_{KL}(P || Q) = H(P, Q) - H(P) = \sum P(x) \log \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} $$

**Why is KL divergence always non-negative? (Gibbs' Inequality)**
Use **Jensen's inequality**: for a convex function $f(x)$, $\mathbb{E}[f(x)] \ge f(\mathbb{E}[x])$.
Since $\log(x)$ is **concave** ($-\log(x)$ is convex), we have:

$$
\begin{aligned}
-D_{KL}(P || Q) &= - \sum P(x) \log \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} \\
&= \sum P(x) \log \frac{Q(x)}{P(x)} \\
&= \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P} \left[ \log \frac{Q(x)}{P(x)} \right] \\
&\le \log \left( \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P} \left[ \frac{Q(x)}{P(x)} \right] \right) \quad (\text{Jensen's inequality}) \\
&= \log \left( \sum P(x) \frac{Q(x)}{P(x)} \right) \\
&= \log \left( \sum Q(x) \right) = \log(1) = 0
\end{aligned}
$$
Therefore $-D_{KL} \le 0 \implies D_{KL} \ge 0$. Equality holds if and only if $P=Q$.

### 2.4 JSD Divergence (Jensen-Shannon Divergence)
KL divergence has two drawbacks: it's asymmetric ($D_{KL}(P||Q) \ne D_{KL}(Q||P)$) and unbounded (which made early GAN training unstable).
**JSD** is the symmetric, smoothed version:
$$ \text{JSD}(P || Q) = \frac{1}{2} D_{KL}(P || M) + \frac{1}{2} D_{KL}(Q || M) $$
where $M = \frac{P+Q}{2}$ is the average distribution.
*   **Properties**: symmetric, and its range is $[0, \log 2]$. This is one reason modern GANs (e.g., StyleGAN) are more stable.

## 3. High-Dimensional Probability: The Collapse of Intuition

We're used to geometric intuition in 2D/3D, but in the 768-dim or 1024-dim spaces common for Embeddings, geometry suffers the **Curse of Dimensionality**.

### 3.1 Concentration of Measure
In high dimensions, volume $V(r) \propto r^d$.
*   **The sphere-shell phenomenon**: most of a high-dimensional ball's volume sits in a **thin shell near the surface**.
*   **Engineering implication**: in high-dimensional vector spaces, all points live "on the surface" — almost none are near the center. That's why LayerNorm and Batch Normalization work — they fight the collapse of data along certain directions.

### 3.2 Distance Failure: Euclidean vs Cosine
In high dimensions, the Euclidean distance between any two randomly sampled points tends to a constant:
$$ \lim_{d \to \infty} \frac{\max dist(x_i, x_j) - \min dist(x_i, x_j)}{\min dist(x_i, x_j)} \to 0 $$
This means **nearest-neighbor search gradually loses discriminative power** under Euclidean distance.

**Cosine Similarity**, by contrast, cares about **direction**. Since high-dimensional vectors tend to be orthogonal (nearly all random vectors are pairwise perpendicular), cosine similarity captures subtle semantic correlations better, and it's immune to magnitude noise (word frequency / text length).

---

## 4. The Law of Large Numbers and Monte Carlo
High-dimensional integrals (e.g., computing marginal probabilities) are a nightmare to solve analytically. The law of large numbers tells us:
$$ \int f(x) p(x) dx = \mathbb{E}[f(x)] \approx \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N f(x_i), \quad x_i \sim p(x) $$
This is the theoretical guarantee for sampling quality in all generative models (Diffusion, VAE).

## 5. Engineering Practice: KL/JSD Comparison and High-Dimensional Distance Visualization

This code contains two experiments, and **automatically configures Chinese fonts** to prevent garbled plots:
1.  **Divergence comparison**: shows KL's asymmetry and JSD's symmetry.
2.  **Distance collapse**: compares how Euclidean vs cosine distance lose discriminative power in high dimensions.

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import scipy.stats
import platform

# === automatic font configuration (fixes Chinese garbled text) ===
system_name = platform.system()
if system_name == 'Windows':
    plt.rcParams['font.sans-serif'] = ['SimHei']  # Windows Hei
elif system_name == 'Darwin':
    plt.rcParams['font.sans-serif'] = ['Arial Unicode MS'] # Mac generic
else:
    # Linux/Colab environments usually need a manually installed Chinese font; fall back to sans-serif here
    # If you're on Linux, we recommend installing 'WenQuanYi Micro Hei'
    plt.rcParams['font.sans-serif'] = ['DejaVu Sans'] 

plt.rcParams['axes.unicode_minus'] = False # fix negative signs showing as boxes

def demo_divergences():
    """
    Experiment 1: KL divergence vs JSD divergence
    """
    # define two Gaussian distributions P and Q
    x = np.linspace(-5, 5, 1000)
    P = scipy.stats.norm.pdf(x, loc=-1.0, scale=1.0)
    Q = scipy.stats.norm.pdf(x, loc=1.0, scale=1.0)
    
    # normalize to prevent numerical errors
    P /= np.sum(P)
    Q /= np.sum(Q)
    M = 0.5 * (P + Q)
    
    # compute KL by hand
    # add 1e-10 to avoid log(0)
    kl_pq = np.sum(P * np.log((P + 1e-10) / (Q + 1e-10)))
    kl_qp = np.sum(Q * np.log((Q + 1e-10) / (P + 1e-10)))
    
    # compute JSD
    jsd = 0.5 * np.sum(P * np.log((P + 1e-10) / (M + 1e-10))) + \
          0.5 * np.sum(Q * np.log((Q + 1e-10) / (M + 1e-10)))
          
    print(f"KL(P||Q) = {kl_pq:.4f}")
    print(f"KL(Q||P) = {kl_qp:.4f} (note the asymmetry)")
    print(f"JSD(P||Q) = {jsd:.4f} (symmetric and bounded)")

def demo_high_dim_metrics():
    """
    Experiment 2: contrast analysis of Euclidean vs Cosine in high dimensions
    """
    dims = np.arange(2, 2000, 100)
    n_samples = 100
    
    euclidean_contrasts = []
    cosine_std_devs = []
    
    for d in dims:
        # randomly sample points (simulating Embeddings)
        X = np.random.randn(n_samples, d)
        
        # compute pairwise distances/similarities
        # 1. Euclidean Distance
        diff = X[:, np.newaxis, :] - X[np.newaxis, :, :]
        dists = np.linalg.norm(diff, axis=-1)
        valid_dists = dists[np.triu_indices(n_samples, k=1)]
        
        # contrast: (Max - Min) / Min
        # the closer to 0, the less distinguishable the distances are
        contrast = (np.max(valid_dists) - np.min(valid_dists)) / np.min(valid_dists)
        euclidean_contrasts.append(contrast)
        
        # 2. Cosine Similarity
        # normalize first
        X_norm = X / np.linalg.norm(X, axis=1, keepdims=True)
        # similarity matrix
        sims = np.dot(X_norm, X_norm.T)
        valid_sims = sims[np.triu_indices(n_samples, k=1)]
        
        # record the standard deviation of cosine similarity (distribution width)
        # we want the distribution to have some width, so it can discriminate
        cosine_std_devs.append(np.std(valid_sims))

    # === visualization ===
    plt.figure(figsize=(12, 5))
    
    # Plot 1: Euclidean Contrast Collapse
    plt.subplot(1, 2, 1)
    plt.plot(dims, euclidean_contrasts, 'b-o', label='Euclidean Contrast')
    plt.title("Euclidean Distance Collapse")
    plt.xlabel("Dimension")
    plt.ylabel("(Max-Min)/Min Distance")
    plt.grid(True)
    plt.text(1000, euclidean_contrasts[-1]+0.2, "trends to 0: cannot distinguish nearest/farthest", color='blue', fontsize=10)
    
    # Plot 2: Cosine vs Euclidean Distribution spread
    # here we compare the trend of their discriminative power (normalized to 0-1 to see the trend)
    plt.subplot(1, 2, 2)
    
    # normalize so they can be compared on the same plot
    euc_trend = np.array(euclidean_contrasts) / np.max(euclidean_contrasts)
    cos_trend = np.array(cosine_std_devs) / np.max(cosine_std_devs)
    
    plt.plot(dims, euc_trend, 'b--', label='Euclidean Contrast (Norm)')
    plt.plot(dims, cos_trend, 'r-', label='Cosine Std Dev (Norm)')
    plt.title("Metric Degradation Comparison")
    plt.xlabel("Dimension")
    plt.ylabel("Normalized Discriminative Power")
    plt.legend()
    plt.grid(True)
    plt.text(100, 0.4, "Cosine also degrades,\nbut usually preserves directional features better", color='red', fontsize=10)
    
    plt.tight_layout()
    plt.show()

if __name__ == "__main__":
    demo_divergences()
    print("-" * 30)
    demo_high_dim_metrics()
```

Reading the experimental results

![probability_information](../images/high_dim_distance.png)

Deep reading of the chart:
* Left plot: Euclidean Distance Collapse
    * Phenomenon: the blue curve shows the distance contrast $\frac{Max_Dist - Min_Dist}{Min_Dist}$ changing with dimension. In low dimensions (e.g., 2D, 10D), the value is large (about 200), meaning "farthest point" and "nearest point" differ significantly.
    * Collapse: once the dimension exceeds 100-200, the curve drops off a cliff and quickly approaches 0.
    * Mathematical meaning: in high-dimensional space (e.g., 1024-dim), $Max_Dist \approx Min_Dist$. In other words, the distances between any two random vectors are nearly identical.
    * Engineering consequence: Euclidean-distance-based KNN (K-nearest neighbors) fails in high dimensions, because "nearest neighbor" loses statistical meaning.
* Right plot: Metric Degradation Comparison
    * Phenomenon: we normalize both metrics' discriminative power and compare. The blue dashed line (Euclidean) hits zero instantly, while the red solid line (cosine std dev) declines more gently.
    * Robustness: although cosine similarity's discriminative power also decreases with dimension, it retains more "directional features". In 1000+ dimensional spaces, the red curve stays above 0, showing cosine similarity still captures faint semantic differences.
Why is Cosine better?: high-dimensional vectors tend to sit on the "sphere shell" surface; the interference from magnitude is eliminated by normalization, leaving only angle information. This explains why OpenAI Embeddings or Vector DBs (e.g., Milvus) recommend Cosine Similarity over L2 distance by default.



Core conclusion:
When building a RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) system, Embedding dimensions are usually as high as 768 or 1536. At this point, geometric intuition is counterintuitive. You must abandon Euclidean distance and instead use cosine similarity or inner products to measure semantic association between vectors.
