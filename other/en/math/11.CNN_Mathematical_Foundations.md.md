### Layer 1: The Essence of Linear Operators — the Convolution Theorem and Hilbert Spaces

Mathematically, the **Convolutional Layer** in CNNs does exploit the optimal coordinate system in Hilbert space.

[Convolution Theorem]:

$f∗g= \mathcal{F}^{−1} (\mathcal{F}(f)⋅\mathcal{F}(g))$

This means: the most complex "translation-invariant" feature extraction in the spatial domain degenerates into the simplest "pointwise multiplication" (a diagonal matrix operator) in the frequency domain.

Hilbert space coordinate transformation:

- The Fourier transform $\mathcal{F}$ is essentially a Unitary Operator on $L^2$ space.
- It doesn't change the amount of information in the signal; it just performs a Change of Basis: from the `position basis` to the `frequency basis`.
- Conclusion: CNN kernels are efficient precisely because they implicitly exploit this coordinate transformation, turning dense matrix operations into sparse (diagonalized) operations.

#### The Physical Meaning of Pooling

From the Hilbert space perspective, as the network goes deeper, the Feature Maps get smaller. This corresponds mathematically to Multi-resolution Analysis (like wavelet transforms).
Each pooling operation essentially discards high-frequency details (positional information) while keeping the low-frequency outline (semantic information), thus describing the object with fewer dimensions (a low-dimensional manifold).

### Layer 2: The Key to Nonlinearity — Why CNN Is More Than Just a Fourier Transform?

If a CNN consisted only of convolutions (Fourier transforms), then no matter how many layers you stack, it would still just be a linear filter $(W_2(W_1(x)) = W_{new}x)$.
The real power of CNN comes from the alternation of convolution (frequency-domain filtering) + ReLU (nonlinear activation):

- **Convolution layer**: exploits the symmetry of the Translation Group in the frequency domain to extract periodic or local features.
- **Activation layer (ReLU)**: breaks frequency isolation. In the frequency domain, ReLU generates high-frequency harmonics, `scattering` low-frequency information to high frequencies, letting the network combine simple low-level features into complex high-level features.

> tips:
> Why must `linear + nonlinear` alternate?
> Without an activation function $\sigma$, two convolutional layers $W_2(W_1(x))$ equal, by associativity, $(W_2W_1)x=W_{new}x$.
> This means a deep network without nonlinearity instantly collapses into a single-layer linear system, losing all ability to combine low-frequency features into high-frequency complex semantics.
> So the mathematical essence of ReLU is a `Frequency Mixer` — an information carrier between frequency bands. It breaks the linear superposition principle of the Fourier transform, letting the model approximate arbitrarily complex non-convex functions.

CNN exploits the Fourier basis to handle translation symmetry (convolution layers), and through nonlinear activations transfers information across different frequency scales, ultimately approximating a complex hierarchical representation of a Lie group.

### **1. What Is a Lie Group?**

- A **Lie group** is a mathematical tool for describing **continuous symmetry**.

  - What is symmetry? E.g., rotation (rotation invariance), translation (translation invariance), mirror reflection, etc. In physics, geometry, and machine learning, **symmetry is key**.
  - A **Lie group** is a set of `continuously transformable` operations. For example:
    - The rotation group **SO(3)**: all rotations around the center of 3D space.
    - The 3D Euclidean group **SE(3)**: rotation + translation combined (e.g., all rigid-body transformations in 3D space).
    - The 2D translation group **T(2)**: all translation operations in 2D space.
- **Lie Algebra** is the `differential` version of a Lie group, describing the **local behavior** of these operations (like the `velocity` or direction of rotation).

---

### **2. What Is a Representation?**

- A **representation** maps the operations of a Lie group (or Lie algebra) onto operations in a **vector space** (like images, point clouds, sequences).

  - For example, the action of a rotation operation **R** on a point **p** in 3D space can be represented as **R · p** (the rotated point).
  - In machine learning, **model parameters** (like convolution kernels, attention weights) learn how to `represent` these symmetry operations.
- An **optimal representation** means:

  - Designing a representation such that **some operations (like translation) simplify to multiplication by a diagonal matrix**, making computation extremely efficient.
  - For example, translation in the frequency domain after a Fourier transform is equivalent to **pointwise multiplication** (this is the core of the convolution theorem!).

### **3. The Bottom Layer of Deep Networks Is a Hierarchical Decomposition of Lie Group Representations**

#### **Core idea:**

> **Deep networks, through their hierarchical structure, decompose the symmetry representations of a Lie group layer by layer, thereby learning high-level features such as translation invariance and rotation invariance.**

#### **Plain explanation:**

1. **Hierarchical decomposition of a Lie group**

   - Consider the rotation-translation group **SE(3)** in 3D space. It can be decomposed as: **SE(3) = SO(3) × T(3)** (rotation + translation).
   - In deep networks (like CNNs), each layer learns the representation of a **subgroup**.
     - **Low layers**: learn translation (e.g., kernels detecting edges, textures).
     - **Middle layers**: learn rotation (e.g., detecting objects in different orientations).
     - **High layers**: learn combined translation and rotation (e.g., detecting an object's position and pose in 3D space).
2. **Benefits of the hierarchy**

   - **Translation invariance**: this is a mathematical property CNN has built-in (determined by convolution).
   - **Rotation invariance**: standard CNN doesn't have mathematical rotation invariance. It can only approximate rotation by learning filters at different angles (redundant filters). True rotation invariance requires Group CNN or Steerable Filters — this is a direct application of Lie group theory to improving modern architectures.

#### **Analogy:**

> Imagine teaching a child to recognize object shapes.
>
> - **Low layers**: first teach edges and textures (translation invariance).
> - **Middle layers**: then teach shapes in different orientations (rotation invariance).
> - **High layers**: finally teach the object's position in 3D space (combined translation and rotation).
>   This is the process by which deep networks hierarchically decompose Lie group representations.

### **4. Geometric Deep Learning: How Transformer Uses the Lie Group SE(3) (Using AlphaFold as an Example)**

#### **Core idea:**

> **Transformer, through self-attention, uses the optimal representation of the Lie group SE(3) to efficiently handle combinations of translation and rotation.**

#### **Plain explanation:**

1. **The SE(3) group**

   - **SE(3)** is the **rigid-body transformation** (rotation + translation) in 3D space. It describes all possible poses of an object in 3D space.
   - **E(3)** might refer to a subgroup (like the rotation subgroup SO(3)), but more commonly it means the **quotient space of SE(3)** (i.e., the `relative pose` left after accounting for translation).
2. **The key to the optimal representation:**

   - In **SE(3)**, the translation operation can be simplified to **pointwise multiplication** in the **frequency domain after a Fourier transform** (similar to the convolution theorem).
   - Transformer's self-attention is essentially learning a **`representation of SE(3)`**, where:
     - **Self-attention** lets the model consider relations among all positions in the sequence simultaneously (similar to handling translation).
     - **Rotation operations** can be modeled through **attention weights** (e.g., in 3D point clouds, attention weights can learn rotation invariance).
3. **Why is it an optimal representation?**

   - Self-attention lets the model **handle translation and rotation simultaneously**, and it's computationally efficient (because attention matrix multiplication can be parallelized).
   - For example, in 3D point clouds, Transformer can:
     - **Translation invariance**: through self-attention, the model is insensitive to translation of the point cloud.
     - **Rotation invariance**: by learning attention weights, the model can adapt to rotation of the point cloud.
   - This is equivalent to using the `optimal form` of the translation operation (pointwise multiplication in the frequency domain) in an SE(3) representation, making computation efficient.

#### **Analogy:**

> Consider a 3D point cloud (like LiDAR data).
>
> - **Translation**: the whole point cloud moves position.
> - **Rotation**: the point cloud rotates around an axis.
> - **Transformer** handles both translation and rotation **simultaneously** through self-attention:
>   - Let every point `attend` to the relative position of all other points (translation).
>   - Let every point `adapt` to the rotation direction of other points (rotation).
>     It's like using the `optimal combination` of translation and rotation in an SE(3) representation to efficiently process 3D data.
