# Game Theory

**Abstract**: In the previous 22 chapters, our assumption was almost always `the environment is static`, and the optimization goal was to find the minimum of a fixed loss function surface. However, in GANs, multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL), or adversarial attacks, the optimization objective depends on the **opponent's** strategy. If the fixed point is no longer a simple minimum but a **Saddle Point**, does traditional gradient descent still work? This chapter reveals the **rotational dynamics** in minimax optimization, uses the **Jacobian matrix** to analyze the source of oscillation, and introduces the **Symplectic Mechanics** perspective to solve training instability.

---

## 1. Static Games and Nash Equilibrium

### 1.1 From Optimization to Equilibrium
In traditional machine learning, we look for parameters $\theta$ that achieve $\min L(\theta)$.
In game theory, even with only two players, the problem becomes finding $(\theta_1, \theta_2)$ such that:
$$
\begin{cases}
\theta_1^* = \arg\min_{\theta_1} L_1(\theta_1, \theta_2^*) \\
\theta_2^* = \arg\min_{\theta_2} L_2(\theta_1^*, \theta_2)
\end{cases}
$$
If $L_1 = -L_2$ (a zero-sum game), this forms a **Min-Max** problem.

### 1.2 Nash Equilibrium (NE)
**Definition**: a strategy profile $(\theta_1^*, \theta_2^*)$ is a Nash equilibrium if no player can benefit by **unilaterally** changing their strategy.

**Existence (Brouwer fixed point theorem)**:
If the strategy space is a compact convex set and the payoff functions are continuous, then a Nash equilibrium must exist.
*   **Intuition**: like throwing a crumpled piece of paper back onto the table — there's always a point that has no vertical displacement.
*   **Challenge**: the theorem only guarantees existence, not that **gradient descent** can find it. In non-convex optimization, Nash equilibria usually correspond to **saddle points**.

## 2. Minimax Optimization and Rotational Dynamics

The most classic minimax problem (and the simplified prototype of GANs) is the bilinear game:
$$ \min_x \max_y f(x, y) = xy $$

### 2.1 The Failure of Gradient Descent-Ascent (GDA)
If we use the naive gradient method:
*   $x$ tries to minimize $xy$ $\Rightarrow$ update along $-\nabla_x f = -y$.
*   $y$ tries to maximize $xy$ $\Rightarrow$ update along $+\nabla_y f = x$.

The dynamics equation (continuous time):
$$
\begin{bmatrix} \dot{x} \\ \dot{y} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -y \\ x \end{bmatrix} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}}_{J} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}
$$

### 2.2 Jacobian Eigenvalue Analysis
The system's stability is determined by the eigenvalues $\lambda$ of the Jacobian matrix $J$.
For $J = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, the characteristic equation is:
$$ \det(J - \lambda I) = \lambda^2 + 1 = 0 \implies \lambda = \pm i $$

*   **Real part Re($\lambda$) < 0**: the system converges stably (has friction).
*   **Real part Re($\lambda$) > 0**: the system diverges.
*   **Real part Re($\lambda$) = 0 and imaginary part Im($\lambda$) $\neq$ 0**: means **pure rotation**.

**Conclusion**: in the simple $xy$ game, the GDA algorithm makes the trajectory draw circles in the phase plane (or spiral out in discrete time), never converging to the Nash equilibrium at the origin $(0,0)$. This is the mathematical root of the common **Oscillation** and **Mode Collapse** in GAN training.

## 3. Dynamics Analysis: Vector Fields and Structural Stability

To understand this deeply, we decompose the **Vector Field** $v(\theta)$ produced by optimization into two parts:
$$ v(\theta) = -\nabla \phi(\theta) + H(\theta) $$

### 3.1 Potential Flow and Hamiltonian Flow
*   **Potential Flow** $-\nabla \phi$: corresponds to the **symmetric part** of the Jacobian (real eigenvalues). It drives the system toward minima, dissipating energy.
*   **Hamiltonian Flow** $H$: corresponds to the **antisymmetric part** of the Jacobian (purely imaginary eigenvalues). It drives the system along contour lines, **conserving energy**.

### 3.2 Structural Stability Analysis
Why is naive GDA dangerous? We need to analyze the **robustness** of the Nash equilibrium $\theta^*$ under small perturbations.

Suppose the system receives a small perturbation near equilibrium $\delta(t) = \theta(t) - \theta^*$; its dynamics follow the linearized equation:
$$ \dot{\delta} = J(\theta^*) \delta $$
where $J$ is the Jacobian. The solution is $\delta(t) = e^{Jt} \delta(0)$.

*   **Structural Instability**:
    For naive GDA (like the $xy$ game), the eigenvalues $\lambda = \pm i$ lie on the imaginary axis.
    Mathematically, this is a **Center**. According to dynamical systems theory, centers are **structurally unstable**. This means any small perturbation $\epsilon$ of the vector field $v$ (e.g., numerical error, random noise, or deviation from zero-sum) can push the eigenvalues into the right half-plane ($\text{Re}(\lambda) > 0$), turning the system from "drawing circles" into "spiral divergence".
    $$ \text{Re}(\lambda) = 0 \xrightarrow{\text{perturbation}} \text{Re}(\lambda) \approx \epsilon > 0 \implies \text{Explosion} $$

*   **Robustness fix**:
    We must introduce a correction term (like the symplectic gradient $\alpha J^T v$), forcing $\text{Re}(\lambda) < -\beta < 0$. Then even with external perturbation $\epsilon$, as long as $\beta > \epsilon$, the system remains Asymptotically Stable.

## 4. Multi-Agent and Bilevel Optimization (Stackelberg Games)

When the game has a sequential order (Leader-Follower), we enter the realm of Stackelberg games. Mathematically this is called **Bilevel Optimization**.

### 4.1 The Mathematical Definition of the Stackelberg Equilibrium
The Leader ($\theta_1$) must anticipate the Follower's ($\theta_2$) optimal response. The objective function is defined as:
$$
\min_{\theta_1} U_{leader}(\theta_1, \theta_2^*(\theta_1))
$$
$$
\text{s.t.} \quad \theta_2^*(\theta_1) = \arg\min_{\theta_2} L_{follower}(\theta_1, \theta_2)
$$
*   **Leader**: meta-learner, hyperparameter optimizer, generator.
*   **Follower**: base learner, network weights, discriminator.

### 4.2 The Hard-Core Derivation: Total Derivatives via the Implicit Function Theorem
How do we take the gradient of the Leader's objective w.r.t. $\theta_1$? The biggest difficulty is that $\theta_2^*$ is an implicit function of $\theta_1$.

By the chain rule, the Leader's Total Gradient is:
$$ \nabla_{\theta_1} U = \frac{\partial U}{\partial \theta_1} + \frac{\partial U}{\partial \theta_2} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{d \theta_2^*}{d \theta_1}}_{\text{Implicit Jacobian}} $$

To compute the implicit Jacobian $\frac{d \theta_2^*}{d \theta_1}$, we use the Follower's optimality condition (KKT conditions):
$$ \nabla_{\theta_2} L_{follower}(\theta_1, \theta_2^*) = 0 $$

Taking the total differential of both sides w.r.t. $\theta_1$:
$$ \nabla_{\theta_1 \theta_2}^2 L_{follower} + \nabla_{\theta_2 \theta_2}^2 L_{follower} \cdot \frac{d \theta_2^*}{d \theta_1} = 0 $$

Rearranging and inverting, we get the famous **implicit gradient formula**:
$$ \frac{d \theta_2^*}{d \theta_1} = - \left[ \nabla_{\theta_2 \theta_2}^2 L_{follower} \right]^{-1} \nabla_{\theta_1 \theta_2}^2 L_{follower} $$

Substituting back, we get the **Stackelberg gradient**:
$$ \nabla_{\theta_1}^{Total} = \nabla_{\theta_1} U - \nabla_{\theta_2} U \cdot \underbrace{\left[ \nabla_{\theta_2 \theta_2}^2 L_{follower} \right]^{-1}}_{\text{Inverse Hessian}} \cdot \nabla_{\theta_1 \theta_2}^2 L_{follower} $$

*   **Computational cost**: directly inverting the Hessian costs $O(N^3)$, which is unacceptable.
*   **Engineering approximation**: in real code (like MAML, DARTS), we usually use a **Neumann series** approximation or **Conjugate Gradient** to estimate the Hessian-Vector Product (HVP), reducing the complexity to $O(N)$.

### 4.3 Cooperation and Betrayal: The LOLA Algorithm
In continuous dilemmas, agents must not only optimize their own loss, but also **shape** the opponent's learning process through the gradient term $\frac{d \theta_{opponent}}{d \theta_{self}}$.
The **LOLA (Learning with Opponent-Learning Awareness)** algorithm uses a Taylor expansion to introduce the above Stackelberg gradient into the update rule, allowing "Tit-for-Tat" cooperation to spontaneously emerge in the prisoner's dilemma.

## 5. Code Practice: GDA Oscillation and Its Correction

We'll simulate the minimax game $f(x, y) = xy$, comparing naive GDA with a corrected GDA.

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# ==========================================
# 1. define the game dynamics
# ==========================================
class GameDynamics:
    def __init__(self, eta=0.1):
        self.eta = eta
        
    def vector_field(self, state):
        x, y = state
        # min_x max_y xy
        # grad_x = y, grad_y = x
        # update_x = -grad_x = -y
        # update_y = +grad_y = x
        return np.array([-y, x])
    
    def symplectic_correction(self, state, lambda_reg=0.5):
        # simulate adding a regularization term or symplectic gradient correction
        # Jacobian J = [[0, -1], [1, 0]]
        # v = [-y, x]
        # J.T @ v = [[0, 1], [-1, 0]] @ [-y, x] = [x, y]
        # Correction term adds a "potential" flow pointing to origin
        x, y = state
        v = self.vector_field(state)
        
        # the correction essentially introduces a real eigenvalue component
        correction = -lambda_reg * np.array([x, y]) 
        return v + correction

# ==========================================
# 2. simulate the trajectories
# ==========================================
def simulate(dynamics, start_pos, steps=200, use_correction=False):
    trajectory = [start_pos]
    state = np.array(start_pos)
    
    for _ in range(steps):
        if use_correction:
            update = dynamics.symplectic_correction(state)
        else:
            update = dynamics.vector_field(state)
        
        state = state + dynamics.eta * update
        trajectory.append(state)
        
    return np.array(trajectory)

# ==========================================
# 3. visualize: phase plane and flow field
# ==========================================
eta = 0.1
game = GameDynamics(eta=eta)

# create a grid for the flow-field visualization
Y, X = np.mgrid[-2:2:20j, -2:2:20j]
U = -Y # dx/dt
V = X  # dy/dt

plt.figure(figsize=(12, 5))

# --- Plot 1: Naive GDA (Standard GAN training) ---
plt.subplot(1, 2, 1)
# draw the vector-field streamlines
plt.streamplot(X, Y, U, V, color='lightgray', density=1.0)
traj_naive = simulate(game, [1.5, 0.0], steps=300, use_correction=False)

plt.plot(traj_naive[:,0], traj_naive[:,1], 'r-', label='Naive GDA', linewidth=2)
plt.scatter([0], [0], c='green', s=100, marker='*', label='Nash Equilibrium')
plt.title("Naive GDA: Orbital Oscillation\n(Pure Imaginary Eigenvalues)")
plt.xlim(-2, 2)
plt.ylim(-2, 2)
plt.xlabel("Player X")
plt.ylabel("Player Y")
plt.legend(loc='upper right')
plt.grid(True, alpha=0.3)

# --- Plot 2: Corrected Dynamics (Symplectic / Regularized) ---
# visualize the corrected vector field
U_corr = U - 0.5 * X
V_corr = V - 0.5 * Y

plt.subplot(1, 2, 2)
plt.streamplot(X, Y, U_corr, V_corr, color='lightgray', density=1.0)
traj_corr = simulate(game, [1.5, 0.0], steps=100, use_correction=True)

plt.plot(traj_corr[:,0], traj_corr[:,1], 'b-', label='Corrected GDA', linewidth=2)
plt.scatter([0], [0], c='green', s=100, marker='*', label='Nash Equilibrium')
plt.title("Corrected GDA: Spiral Convergence\n(Added Real Eigenvalue Component)")
plt.xlim(-2, 2)
plt.ylim(-2, 2)
plt.xlabel("Player X")
plt.ylabel("Player Y")
plt.legend(loc='upper right')
plt.grid(True, alpha=0.3)

plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

### 5.1 Analysis of the Experimental Results

![game dynamic](../images/game_dynamic.png)

Reading the figure:
The figure above shows the evolution trajectories of the strategy space $(x,y)$ under two different dynamics rules, starting from the same position $(1.5,0.0)$. The gray arrows in the background represent the vector field (the opposite direction of the gradient).

Left plot: Naive GDA

- Phenomenon: the red trajectory forms closed circles (actually drifting slightly outward due to discrete step error), always rotating around the central green star (the Nash equilibrium) but never approaching it.
- Mathematical essence: the Jacobian's eigenvalues are purely imaginary $\lambda = \pm i$.
This is a Conservative Field, like an undamped pendulum in physics. The system's `energy` (the radius from the origin) is conserved. $$\frac{d}{dt}(x^2 + y^2) = 0 $$

Deep learning insight: this explains why the original GAN training is extremely unstable. The generator and discriminator play a `cat-and-mouse game` in strategy space, causing the loss to oscillate and never converge.

Right plot: Corrected GDA

- Phenomenon: the blue trajectory shows a Spiral Convergence pattern, quickly and smoothly converging to the central Nash equilibrium.

- Mathematical essence: by introducing a regularization term (like Symplectic Gradient Adjustment or Gradient Penalty), we turn the eigenvalues into complex numbers with a negative real part: $\lambda = -\alpha \pm i$.
The negative real part $-\alpha$ acts like Friction or Damping in a physical system. It continuously dissipates the system's energy, forcing particles to fall into the lowest energy point.
Deep learning insight: using Gradient Penalty (WGAN-GP) or Spectral Normalization in GANs essentially turns the left plot's rotational field into the right plot's convergent field.

## 6. Summary

Introducing the game-theoretic view turns machine learning from `mountain climbing` into `dancing in a whirlwind`.

- The imaginary part of the Jacobian is the culprit behind oscillation.

- Nash equilibria are often saddle points, not minima.

- To make GANs or MARL converge, we can't rely on gradient descent alone — we must manipulate the system's dynamical properties through regularization or symplectic algorithms.
